A visual plaque score with four possible values correlates with serum CRP at a Spearman coefficient of 0.893. With SAA it is also 0.893, with IL-6 0.883. These numbers come from Sharei and colleagues at Shiraz University, who studied 131 client-owned dogs (Sharei et al., 2026), and they make a condition I meet every week as bad breath and tartar look as if it were shouting in the blood. My first reaction was suspicion. In field data from mixed-breed dogs, an ordinal score and a blood protein are rarely this tightly linked.

Suspicion is not a verdict on the study. The paper is open access and the methods are detailed, which is what lets a reader see its limits at all.

## How "healthy" was defined

The design is cross-sectional: of the client-owned dogs presenting to the Small Animal Hospital of Shiraz University, 70 were counted as healthy and 61 as having periodontal disease. The split rests entirely on two scores assigned by eye. The plaque index (PI) and the gingival index (GI) each run from 0 to 3. A dog with PI and GI both at zero was healthy; a dog with PI or GI of at least one was diseased.

That choice has two consequences. First, the definition of the disease group is the same variable that is later correlated with the biomarkers, so the group difference and the score-biomarker correlation count the same information twice. Second, the threshold for "disease" is very low: a little plaque on a tooth puts the dog in the diseased group. The dogs were also examined awake, without sedation, and no probing, attachment loss, tooth mobility or radiography was recorded. The authors give ethical reasons and list this among their limitations: what was measured is plaque and gingival inflammation, not tissue destruction.

The lack of probing also affects the healthy group. A mouth that looks clean from the outside can still hide deep pockets. Rawlinson and colleagues, in 2011, scored every tooth root for gingivitis and attachment loss in 38 dogs and drew blood again four weeks after treatment. After Bonferroni correction, the strongest links with attachment loss were platelet count (r = 0.54), creatinine (r = −0.49) and the before-after difference in CRP (r = 0.40) (Rawlinson et al., 2011). In a study that measured attachment loss, the best CRP-related correlation was about 0.40; in one that did not, it is 0.89.

The two numbers cannot be compared directly. One is a before-after difference and the other a single time point, and one study has 38 dogs and the other 131. Still, the gap raises the question of why a visual plaque score predicts CRP so well. I suspect part of the answer lies in how the measurements and groups were built.

## Where such a tight correlation could come from

The methods give a few clues. The canine IL-6 ELISA used here has a detection range of 0.156 to 1.00 ng/mL, which is narrow. The CRP and SAA assays are kits from a different manufacturer, and the SAA sensitivity is printed as "0.156 mg/mL", a figure that looks very high against usual SAA concentrations and may be a unit error. So three inflammation markers were measured with three different assays. Yet their correlations with PI all fall between 0.883 and 0.893. The between-group effect size is also reported as identical for the three: r = 0.86 for IL-6, CRP and SAA.

Identical effect sizes in three different proteins are possible, but not what I would expect. An effect size like this means the two groups hardly overlap. Among dogs of every breed, age and lifestyle, such a clean separation makes me think something unmeasured may have gone into building the groups. If the diseased dogs came to the hospital for another complaint and the healthy ones for a routine check, for example, that alone could create a difference; the paper does not give reasons for presentation. The authors say they excluded systemic disease on owner-reported history and clinical examination, and that subclinical conditions were not screened comprehensively. That may not be enough to explain 0.89 in 131 dogs, but it is a possible contributor.

There is also a biological reason why the three markers look alike, and the authors give it in their introduction: IL-6 is the cytokine that stimulates the liver to make acute-phase proteins, and CRP and SAA rise largely under its direction. So the three measurements are not three independent witnesses but two stages of one signal. A dog with high IL-6 is expected to have high CRP and SAA, and similar correlations are no surprise. That explains the consistency; it does not explain why the correlation is as high as 0.89.

The difference between the plaque and gingival scores is worth a thought too. The plaque index correlates with the biomarkers at 0.88 to 0.89, whereas the gingival index stays at 0.68 to 0.69 (for MDA 0.449 against 0.343, for TAC −0.266 against −0.222). The correlation between the two scores is 0.606. The authors interpret this as microbial plaque load being more closely related to systemic inflammation than gum inflammation is, and they do not claim direct causation. What strikes me as odd is that the gingival index measures the host response, and a closer link to systemic inflammation would have been the expected result. Plaque coming out ahead may have something to do with its being part of the group definition, and with age as well (the plaque index correlates with age at r = 0.266, the gingival index at 0.189).

The multivariable models are striking as well. After adjusting for age, sex, body weight and skull type, diseased dogs had 2.43 times the IL-6 (95% CI 2.246 to 2.629), 2.85 times the CRP (2.541 to 3.189) and 2.43 times the SAA. Confidence intervals this narrow mean within-group variability is low. For an acute-phase protein such as CRP in client-owned, mixed-breed dogs, that surprises me. This is my interpretation; without the full data table I pass no judgement.

A few notes on pre-analytical conditions are due. Blood was drawn from the jugular vein between eight and twelve in the morning, and the serum was spun at 3,000 rpm for 15 minutes and stored at −20 °C until analysis. Whether the dogs had fasted is not stated in the text. Variation coefficients are quoted from the manufacturers: under 8 percent within and 10 percent between runs for the ELISAs, 3.4 and 4.2 percent for TAC. What I could not read is whether the three ELISAs were run on one plate or on different days, or whether healthy and diseased samples were mixed across plates. If diseased and healthy samples were run on different plates or days, part of the group difference could be a plate difference.

The authors' own caution deserves credit. The abstract speaks of "coordinated systemic inflammatory activation", whereas the discussion says plainly that the findings should not be read as a systemic consequence of the disease, only as changes that exist at the same time. A cross-sectional design permits nothing else, but their restraint throughout the text matches the word "associated" in the title. The same warning is given for CRP and IL-6: both are non-specific markers that rise in processes outside the mouth.

Sex was related neither to the biomarkers nor to the periodontal scores (all P > 0.05), while the distribution of skull type between the groups was borderline (P = 0.055). That matters because brachycephalic dogs, with their crowded teeth, are prone to periodontal trouble, and a borderline imbalance in skull type is exactly the kind of thing that can sneak into a group comparison. The diseased group was older than the healthy group (P = 0.023), lighter (P = 0.001) and had a larger share of small dogs (P < 0.001). Age was an independent predictor of IL-6 (P = 0.009) and CRP (P = 0.019). It is good that these were adjusted for, but body size and weight carry partly the same information, and tartar build-up in small breeds is a known tendency anyway.

The only sample-size calculation is a post hoc sensitivity analysis: with 70 against 61 dogs, a standardised effect size of 0.50 and α = 0.05, the power is 80.9 percent. The authors do not count this as an a priori calculation, since the number of dogs depended on who came to the hospital. Because the observed effect sizes are 0.86, power is not a problem for the main comparisons. It becomes one when the dogs are split into severity categories: the authors say some severity groups held very few animals, which lowers power, especially for the oxidative markers.

## One disease, several faces

The part of the study I found most useful is the oxidative side. In the adjusted model MDA was only about 6 percent higher in diseased dogs (ratio 1.059, 95% CI 1.040 to 1.079), and total antioxidant capacity (TAC) about 11.5 percent lower (0.885, 0.797 to 0.983, P = 0.023). Two- to threefold changes in inflammatory markers against a few percent in oxidative markers. The authors read this as inflammation being the dominant response and oxidative imbalance a secondary one.

That reading is a comparison from a single blood sample. Putting fold changes measured on different scales next to each other (SAA against MDA) does not show that one is more dominant than the other. Serum MDA by the TBARS method is known for limited specificity, and a small difference may owe more to the assay than to biology.

Other studies complicate the picture. Turgut and Kurtdede compared 52 dogs, 26 with gingivitis and 26 with periodontitis. Serum total oxidant status (TOS), total antioxidant status (TAS) and IL-6 were higher in periodontitis, whereas IL-1β, MCP-1, caveolin-1 and neopterin were higher in gingivitis (Turgut and Kurtdede, 2026). So the profile changes with stage: mediator-heavy early on, oxidative and IL-6-heavy later. Sharei's study pools gingivitis and periodontitis into one "disease" category; split apart, they might have produced an interesting pattern.

Peştean and colleagues in Cluj-Napoca looked at saliva from 22 dogs. Compared with controls, MMP8 was 1.2-fold higher in the mild tartar group, 2-fold in the moderate group and 3.7-fold in the severe group; 8-OHdG rose up to 8.6-fold in the severe group, while MDA roughly doubled (Peştean et al., 2024). The marker of oxidative DNA damage moved far more than the marker of lipid peroxidation. That hints that the 6 percent MDA rise in Sharei's serum may reflect the sensitivity of MDA more than an absence of oxidative stress. Local samples (saliva, gingival crevicular fluid) may be more sensitive than systemic ones, which fits the GCF-serum correlations of Turgut and Kurtdede but is not proven.

Kurtdede and colleagues, in 2025, reported from a fifty-dog treatment trial that MDA fell and TAC rose within seven days (Kurtdede et al., 2025). From the abstract, this is a comparison of clinoptilolite and antibiotic protocols, and beyond an uncontrolled time effect I cannot say more. It does show that redox markers move quickly with treatment.

## After the tartar is gone

The question this study cannot answer is whether the high values come back down. The authors call for longitudinal work themselves. Rawlinson's design tried it, and the CRP change four weeks after treatment was related to disease severity. In diseased dogs that are going to have a dental cleaning anyway, measuring the same five markers with the same kits before and after would make every dog its own control and remove the confounding by age and breed.

Such a design would also settle a smaller puzzle: whether the weaker oxidative signal, 6 percent for MDA and 11.5 percent for TAC, is real biology or a limit of serum assays. Kurtdede's trial suggests those markers respond within a week, so a four-week window should be long enough to see them move.

If CRP really falls substantially within four weeks, then much of the 0.893 comes from the mouth. If it does not, part of the elevation belongs to who the patients are. The way to find out is to clean the mouth and draw the blood again.
