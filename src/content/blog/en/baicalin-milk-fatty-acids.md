In the control cows, oleic acid (C18:1n9c) made up 22.41% of milk fatty acids. In the same cows on 25 g of baicalin a day it made up 24.80%. That is a gain of about 2.4 percentage points, roughly 11% in relative terms, and stearic acid (C18:0) moved the other way, from 10.90% to 9.49%. A group at China Agricultural University, led by Shen, published these numbers in *Microbiology Spectrum* and wrapped them in hundreds of metabolites and a list of milk bacteria to build a single mechanism. My reading is that the story holds up for the first two numbers and becomes a hypothesis after that.

## From 22.41 to 24.80

Baicalin is a flavonoid, extracted from the roots of *Scutellaria baicalensis*, with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity that is well documented in other settings. The authors wanted to know whether it could be a feed additive that improves milk fat. The design is a crossover: six Holstein cows, about 84 days into lactation and weighing around 680 kg, each went through two 28-day periods, one on a control diet and one on the same diet plus baicalin, in random order. Each period had 21 days of adaptation and 7 days of sampling, with milk and blood collected on day 26. So the dataset is six animals and twelve milk samples, from a single day in each period.

A crossover has an obvious virtue: every cow is her own control, so the fact that one cow simply gives richer milk than another does not contaminate the comparison. The weak points are sample size and timing. There is one sampling day per period and one milk sample per day. The fatty acids were compared with paired t-tests, which do not model period or sequence effects. The trial ran from July to September 2023, so both periods fall in the hot season, and how much the animals ate or how their yield moved is not discussed in the parts of the paper I read.

The fatty acid table has 23 rows. Five of them cross the 0.05 line: lauric acid (C12:0) fell from 3.84 to 3.41 (p = 0.011), stearic acid fell from 10.90 to 9.49 (p = 0.035), oleic acid rose (p = 0.018), linoleic acid (C18:2n6c) rose from 3.29 to 3.79 (p = 0.035), and alpha-linolenic acid (C18:3n3) rose from 0.24 to 0.29 (p = 0.014). Palmitic acid (C16:0), the dominant fatty acid in the milk, did not move: 37.16 in controls against 36.48 on baicalin, p = 0.379. Total unsaturated fatty acids went up and total saturated ones came down.

With 23 independent tests at a 0.05 threshold, one false positive is expected by chance. Five hits are more than that, and what reassures me is the direction. Medium-chain saturates and stearic acid shrink while the eighteen-carbon unsaturates grow; random noise rarely agrees with itself this neatly. I should add a caution on the arithmetic, though. Fatty acids are expressed as percentages that add up to 100, so when one rises another must fall. The oleic gain and the stearic loss are probably one finding counted twice. The methods mention false-discovery correction for the metabolite lists but not for the fatty acid comparison.

The pattern has a biological shape worth noticing. The mammary gland builds its own fatty acids from acetate and butyrate, covering the short and medium chains up to about fourteen carbons and part of palmitic acid, and this is where genes like FASN and ACACA work. Everything of eighteen carbons and longer arrives from the blood, that is, from the feed and from body fat. Lauric acid fell and the eighteen-carbon unsaturates rose, which could mean that de novo synthesis eased off while uptake from blood gained weight. Yet myristic acid (C14:0) sits at 11.45 on baicalin and 11.28 in controls, and palmitic acid stayed put. If synthesis had really been throttled I would expect those two to budge as well. Whatever baicalin did, it did it within a narrow window of the profile.

Whether any of this matters on a farm is a separate question. A 2.4-point gain in oleic acid in six cows does not guarantee a gain of the same size in a bulk tank. The direction, though, agrees with several other flavonoid-rich feeds, which I come back to below.

## Milk bacteria and a correlation built on twelve samples

Having found a shift, the authors ask why. They sequenced the 16S rRNA gene of the milk microbiota. The richness estimate Chao1 differed between groups, while the Shannon and Simpson indices did not. In the Bray-Curtis ordination the two groups did not separate, and the ANOSIM statistic stayed at 0.096. Even so, LEfSe analysis picked out 13 discriminating taxa, and four genera were then linked to fatty acid proportions: two positively with unsaturated fatty acids, two negatively.

On the metabolomics side the picture moves at two different speeds. Serum separated cleanly between the groups in the PCA and yielded 122 differential metabolites. Milk did not separate in the PCA, yet 177 metabolites passed the filter. The most discussed milk metabolite is alpha-ketoglutarate (alpha-KG), higher on baicalin. Drawing on the literature, the authors propose that alpha-KG activates stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), the enzyme that converts stearic acid into oleic acid. It sounds neat: stearic acid is down, oleic acid is up. But SCD1 was measured at neither the mRNA nor the protein level. What the data contain is a swap between two fatty acids and one metabolite sitting in the milk.

The labelling of pathways deserves a comment too. The authors set apart ten pathways in the milk data as "fatty acid metabolism-related". Glycerophospholipid metabolism and fatty acid biosynthesis genuinely belong there. But biosynthesis of amino acids, biosynthesis of cofactors, the TCA cycle and carbon metabolism sit under the same heading. Almost every energy pathway in a cell touches fatty acids at some point. Under that broad definition 19 metabolites count as "related", and their correlations with fatty acid proportions are then examined. The screening threshold (VIP above 1, fold change above 1.2 or below 0.8, corrected p below 0.05) is reasonable. The pathway label is generous.

The central claim comes from the Mantel tests. Serum metabolites were linked to milk metabolites at a single point, ureidosuccinic acid, and that metabolite correlated with no fatty acid proportion. Milk bacteria, in contrast, were tied to many milk metabolites and to fatty acids. From this the authors conclude that baicalin changes milk fatty acid composition through the milk microbiota more than through host metabolism.

I find that inference weak, for two reasons. The first is numerical: Spearman correlations on twelve samples turn significant easily and disappear just as easily. The second is more basic, because the direction of the correlation is unknown. Does fatty acid composition shape the bacteria, or do the bacteria shape the fatty acids? The lipid environment of milk puts selective pressure on whatever can grow in it. On top of this the authors acknowledge that they neither controlled nor monitored environmental sources of contamination (milking equipment, air, teat skin). Milk is a low-biomass sample, which makes that omission a serious source of uncertainty in the microbiota result.

The serum arm gets less space in the authors' narrative, yet I find it more interesting, since it is the only sample type that separated. Glutamate and carnitine were lower on baicalin, and a bile acid, glycochenodeoxycholic acid, was higher. The authors link the lower glutamate to FoxO signalling and easier lipid breakdown, and the bile acid to fatty acid oxidation through FXR. Even here the logic reads "this metabolite could switch on that receptor", with no link in the chain measured. Still, a serum profile that shifts under baicalin next to a milk profile that does not in the PCA is a clue: something happened inside the animal, but it may not suffice to explain a small change in milk fatty acids.

## The rumen door stayed shut

Most of the fat in milk reflects what happens in the rumen, where bacteria saturate the unsaturated fatty acids the cow eats (biohydrogenation), plus what the mammary gland does with the remainder. Baicalin is known for antibacterial activity, and here it was fed by mouth. In that situation, an effect on rumen microbes is the first place to look when explaining a milk fat shift. In this study no rumen fluid was taken. Volatile fatty acids and rumen microbiota were not measured. The discussion admits it plainly: biohydrogenation is a major determinant of milk fatty acid composition, and without rumen data a direct mammary effect cannot be told apart from an indirect rumen-mediated one.

My trouble is that this admission sits uneasily with the title and abstract. The title leans on "milk metabolism and the microbiome" and suggests causation, while the data never test whether what reaches the milk was shaped in the rumen. Serum 3-hydroxybutyric acid went up, and the authors read it as a PPARα signal that promotes fatty acid oxidation. As a veterinarian I know that metabolite as a ketosis marker in early lactation. The text does not connect it with ketosis, and these cows are in their third month. Yet the fact that one molecule can be read here as "fat burning" shows how flexible the interpretation is.

Nor does the paper report whether the 25-gram dose was chosen for a reason, or how much baicalin or its metabolites reached the milk. If the compound reaches the udder, a direct effect on the gland becomes plausible; if it does not, the explanation lives elsewhere. A targeted measurement in a milk sample would separate the two.

## Other trials from the same family

The study does not stand alone. Zhao and colleagues gave eight cows up to 150 g/day of citrus peel extract and saw higher proportions of conjugated linoleic acid, along with more phosphatidylethanolamine and phosphatidylcholine in the milk (Zhao et al., 2022). The baicalin cows also had more phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine in their milk. Seeing the same phospholipid signal with two different flavonoid sources could point to a family effect. It could equally reflect the same analytical platform highlighting the same class.

Tilahun and colleagues fed amla fruit (*Phyllanthus emblica*) to eight cannulated cows, and total saturated fatty acids fell while EPA and DHA rose. Yao and colleagues fed heat-stressed cows yellow wine lees, and the share of unsaturated fatty acids in the milk went up while the saturated share went down. Both involve feeds loaded with phenolics and flavonoids, and in both the question of mechanism leaves the rumen and gut leg incompletely closed.

There is also a counterexample. Benchaar and Chouinard used four cows and found no effect of cinnamaldehyde or condensed tannins on the milk fatty acid profile; yucca saponin trimmed C6:0 (2.71 against 2.95) and C8:0 (1.66 against 1.89) only slightly. Not every plant secondary metabolite disturbs biohydrogenation, and "flavonoids improve milk fat" cannot be applied to every compound.

In a separate trial with citrus flavonoids, Zhao and colleagues saw the effect gather in the gut rather than the milk: somatic cell count and serum cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) fell, and blood lipopolysaccharide dropped (Zhao et al., 2023). Flavonoids in cattle seem to work frequently through the gut and inflammation. I have no data to exclude the possibility that the fatty acid shift from baicalin was set in motion along that route before it reached the udder.

## The next experiment

If I were to run this study again, I would add three things. The first is rumen fluid, for volatile fatty acids and for 16S alike. The second is RT-qPCR for lipogenic genes such as SCD1, FASN and SREBF1 in milk cells or a mammary biopsy, because the alpha-KG claim is a question that these genes can answer. The third is more animals. A 2.4-point rise in oleic acid in six cows may well be real. But to say which tissue produced it, at least one of the measurements the hypothesis itself proposes has to be on the table.
