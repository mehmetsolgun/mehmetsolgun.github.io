Take sperm from a low-fertility bull, add extracellular vesicles that were purified from the seminal plasma of a proven-fertility bull, and the sperm start to move differently. Add vesicles from low-fertility bulls, or nothing at all, and that shift does not appear. A group from the University of Milan, the Italian National Research Council and a national bull centre reported this in Animal Reproduction Science (Lange-Consiglio et al., 2026). Then they looked at the microRNAs (miRNAs) inside the vesicles, expecting to find what made the difference. They found no significant difference between the two fertility groups.

That result pokes a hole in a tidy story. According to it, seminal plasma vesicles (extracellular vesicles, EVs) carry miRNAs, sperm pick them up, and part of the fertility gap between bulls is born in that handover. The story is plausible and it has been told in several papers. The new data cut it just before the final link.

## What changed in the tube

A mature sperm cell is close to transcriptionally silent. It makes almost no new RNA, yet it carries a surprisingly rich RNA population, and part of that population is acquired from outside, through vesicles, during the final stages of spermatogenesis and the passage through the epididymis (Alves et al., 2020; Silva et al., 2026). So the vesicles of seminal plasma look attractive for two reasons. They might serve as a biomarker source for fertility. They might also be a tool that gives sperm something functional.

The interest has a practical root. Chauhan and colleagues remind readers in their introduction that an ejaculate is roughly 5% sperm and 95% seminal plasma (Chauhan et al., 2024). Bulls are chosen by breeding soundness criteria, and even so, fertility on the farm differs widely from one bull to the next. If a routine semen evaluation does not predict fertility well enough, looking into the plasma is a reasonable second step.

Lange-Consiglio and colleagues isolated seminal plasma vesicles from proven-fertility and low-fertility Holstein-Friesian bulls by ultracentrifugation. They checked the preparations with nanoparticle tracking analysis, Western blotting and transmission electron microscopy, measured sperm movement with computer-assisted sperm analysis, and profiled miRNAs by small RNA sequencing.

The first finding sets the tone. Conventional semen parameters did not differ significantly between the two groups of bulls, so a standard semen report could not have told them apart in this data set. The one measurable difference was vesicle concentration, which was significantly higher in proven-fertility bulls. When low-fertility sperm were co-incubated with vesicles from proven-fertility bulls, their kinematic profile separated from both untreated sperm and sperm incubated with vesicles from low-fertility bulls.

I only had the abstract. The full text is not open access, and the abstract gives neither the number of bulls nor the size of the kinematic shift. I would look for both numbers first, because everything that follows depends on how large and how replicated that shift is.

A second question comes to mind at once. Proven-fertility bulls had more vesicles per unit of seminal plasma. When their vesicles were added to low-fertility sperm, was the dose matched? The abstract does not say. If the doses were not matched, some of the kinematic effect could reflect quantity rather than quality.

## The difference that is not in the sequencing data

The sequencing results diverge in two places. In spermatozoa, 16 miRNAs were differentially expressed between proven-fertility and low-fertility bulls. In the vesicles, the miRNA cargo did not differ significantly between the groups.

Put the two lines next to each other and the puzzle is clear. Sperm miRNAs separate the groups. The vesicles that supposedly deliver miRNAs to sperm do not. Either the sperm miRNA differences come from sources other than seminal vesicles, or the vesicle differences exist but this study could not detect them. The authors lean on the first reading. They write that the vesicle effect cannot be explained by a fertility-specific miRNA cargo and that other vesicle-associated mechanisms may be involved. They did not investigate what those mechanisms are.

I would not close the second possibility. When the true difference between groups is modest and each group has few animals, sequencing returns "no difference" as a matter of course. This is a result worth publishing, and it should be read as "this cargo did not discriminate in this data set". Reading it as "miRNAs play no role" goes further than the authors do, and their own sentence is cautious in just the same way.

The question of where sperm miRNAs come from matters here. According to the review by Silva and colleagues, the miRNA profile of a mature sperm cell is the combined result of spermatogenesis, epididymal maturation and environmental exposure. The epididymal epithelium secretes epididymosomes that hand selected miRNAs to maturing spermatozoa, and that transfer contributes to the acquisition of motility, capacitation and fertilising ability (Silva et al., 2026). Seminal plasma vesicles meet the sperm at ejaculation, after most of that process is over. The 16 miRNA differences in the sperm could therefore have been set in the testis and epididymis, long before the vesicles arrive, and seminal plasma vesicles might do something else afterwards. That is an inference of mine, not a hypothesis the study tested.

## The same question in other bulls

Chauhan and colleagues asked a similar question in Sahiwal bulls and came back with a different answer (Chauhan et al., 2024). Their design is instructive about how this field works. At the artificial breeding centre of India's National Dairy Research Institute, 32 bulls were assessed, and their conception rates followed a normal distribution with a mean of 44.5% and a standard deviation of 7.9%. Bulls more than one standard deviation below the mean were called low fertility and those more than one above it high fertility. From these, six bulls with more than 50 insemination records each were picked: three high-fertility animals (conception rates between 55.8% and 59.6%) and three low-fertility animals (29.2% to 30.4%).

That is an extreme-phenotype design, with a wide gap between groups and three animals in each. The vesicles were isolated by size-exclusion chromatography, and sequencing identified 310 miRNAs. With a p value below 0.05 and a log fold change above one, 41 remained, 20 higher and 21 lower in the high-fertility group, among them bta-miR-1246, bta-miR-195, bta-miR-339b and bta-miR-199b. I could not see in the text whether the threshold was corrected for multiple testing. In spermatozoa, qPCR showed bta-miR-195 to be 80% higher in the high-fertility group.

Three bulls against three, selected at the poles, is a favourable arrangement for catching a vesicle miRNA difference. There is one more detail. For sequencing, the vesicle fractions of each group were pooled. The authors write that they did this to reduce variation between individuals and to capture the maximum number of miRNAs, and they note themselves that analyses with more bulls are needed. Pooling hides whether a miRNA is raised in all three bulls or strongly in just one. For a biomarker claim that matters, because the clinic wants to classify a single bull.

The Sahiwal study differs in another respect. Vesicle size and concentration did not differ between its groups, and the authors concluded that fertility was not influenced by those two properties. In the new study, concentration was higher in the proven-fertility bulls. The count discriminates in one breed and not the other. For miRNA the picture flips: it separates the groups in Sahiwal vesicles and not in Holstein-Friesian ones. The new study's groups are called "proven fertility" and "low fertility", and the abstract does not say how that line was drawn. If it is less extreme than in the Sahiwal design, small miRNA differences in vesicles would be no surprise.

Put the two studies side by side and several things differ at once: breed (Sahiwal against Holstein-Friesian), isolation method (size-exclusion against ultracentrifugation), the definition of fertility and the statistical thresholds. Which of these produced the discrepancy cannot be decided from abstracts. How the isolation method shapes the miRNA data is a real argument in the field, and I mention it as a possibility, not as an explanation anyone has shown.

The scrotal heat stress study of Alves and colleagues looks at the problem from another side (Alves et al., 2020). Three Nelore bulls wore scrotal bags for 96 hours and three served as controls. Seven days later, only three miRNAs in seminal plasma small vesicles had gone down (miR-23b-5p, miR-489 and miR-1248). Twenty-one days later, 21 sperm miRNAs had changed. The vesicles moved little, the sperm moved a lot. That roughly fits the new paper, in which the sperm profile separates the groups and the vesicle cargo does not. With three animals per group I treat it as a hypothesis.

Small sample sizes seem normal in this literature. Pohler and colleagues sequenced serum vesicle miRNAs to tell apart cows that kept a pregnancy from cows that lost the embryo between days 17 and 30, using four animals per group and day (Pohler et al., 2017). They identified 214 miRNAs in serum and reported 27 differentially abundant mature miRNAs. This is a useful yardstick for how biomarker claims start in this field: with data of that scale, and they stand or fall when an independent cohort is tested.

## The tube is an intervention too

The least discussed finding is, to my eye, the most interesting one. Merely incubating sperm in the tube, without any vesicles, was associated with a larger number of changes in the sperm miRNA profile. Sperm incubated together with vesicles from proven-fertility bulls changed less.

According to the review, bovine sperm lose motility, membrane integrity and fertilising ability quickly at 41 to 43 degrees, while physiological temperatures of 38.5 to 39 degrees preserve function (Silva et al., 2026). The abstract does not give the incubation temperature or the duration, and that alone is a reminder that the tube is an intervention. Here is how I read it, and I want to be clear that this is my reading and not the authors' conclusion. A sperm cell sitting in medium loses or reshuffles part of its RNA, and fertile vesicles may slow that drift. If that is right, the job of a vesicle might be to protect what the sperm already carries and not to hand it new miRNAs. The abstract neither supports nor refutes this idea. The protection could also have nothing to do with miRNA, since the protein and lipid content of the vesicles was not examined here.

Haupenthal and colleagues added epididymosomes, vesicles of epididymal origin, to a cryopreservation extender (Haupenthal et al., 2026). After thawing, the sperm showed higher motility and better plasma membrane integrity, 28 miRNAs were higher or detected only in the epididymosome group, and the rate of hatched blastocysts in in vitro embryo production went up. The epididymosome pool came from the fluid of 15 bulls and the sperm from six, so individual identity was lost in the pool. This is more evidence that vesicles do something to sperm. Which component does it, miRNA or something else, is not separated by that experiment either.

The review by Silva and colleagues on heat stress and the bull sperm transcriptome rounds out the picture (Silva et al., 2026). It describes vesicles from seminal plasma and epididymis as carriers of proteins, lipids, miRNAs, mRNAs and long non-coding RNAs that influence sperm motility, capacitation and fertilisation potential. Which piece of the package produces which effect is a separate question that nobody in this group of papers answers.

For a practitioner, the most usable item may be an unexpected one: vesicle concentration. Routine parameters could not separate the groups, but the count was higher in proven-fertility bulls. It is a number that nanoparticle tracking can deliver without any sequencing. Whether it is a good enough discriminator cannot be judged from data of this size, but it would be a cheap hypothesis for the next, larger study to test.

If the difference in the tube does not come from miRNA, the next place to look is the protein and lipid cargo of the vesicles. That experiment has not been done yet.
