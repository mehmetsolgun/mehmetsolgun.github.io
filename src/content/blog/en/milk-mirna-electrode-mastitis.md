By sequencing, miR-223 looked 62 times higher in milk from cows with subclinical mastitis. Measured with the electrode, the difference between healthy and subclinical cows did not reach significance. That mismatch is the line in the paper by Shafique and colleagues, published in *ACS Measurement Science Au* in June 2026, that held my attention longest (Shafique et al., 2026). The authors offer an explanation and it is a reasonable one. It also sets the terms for how the rest of the paper should be read.

The idea is simple. In subclinical mastitis the milk somatic cell count (SCC) rises, but SCC also moves with stress; culture is slow and costly; PCR cannot tell dead bacteria from live ones. Measuring the host response might work better, and milk miRNAs are candidates for it. The authors first found the up-regulated miRNAs by sequencing, then designed a DNA sensor on a gold electrode for each one, then tried the sensors on RNA extracted from milk. Every step contains a small number worth looking at.

## One pool, two libraries

For sequencing, 35 cows from a herd of 45 Holstein-Friesians were screened at milking on three consecutive days. Cows with SCC under 200,000 cells/mL and no clinical signs counted as healthy; those between 200,000 and 500,000 counted as subclinical. Ten animals were chosen, five of each. RNA was extracted from 4 mL of filtered skimmed milk with the QIAGEN exoRNeasy kit. Individual samples yielded too little RNA, so the groups were pooled: one healthy pool, one subclinical pool, one library for each.

The consequence is that the table reporting 62.40-fold for miR-223, 30.57-fold for miR-221 and 2.79-fold for miR-146b, each with p < 0.001, rests on read counts from two libraries. There are no biological replicates. The authors say themselves that pooling can mask variation between animals. The read counts show a second asymmetry: after UMI correction the subclinical library retained 851,171 reads and the healthy one 602,616, and the reads assignable to known bovine miRNAs were 292,512 and 151,442. Depth differs about two-fold, and the text does not make clear whether the fold changes were adjusted for it.

There is also an arithmetic inconsistency. The text says miR-223 rose from fewer than 400 reads in healthy milk to 2,279 in subclinical milk, and calls that roughly 62-fold. Divide 2,279 by 62.4 and the healthy pool has about 37 reads, not fewer than 400. I do not know which number is misprinted. If it is 37 reads, then the criterion of "detectable baseline expression in healthy milk", one of the authors' two selection rules, stands on thin ground.

For the authors the three miRNAs share two features: present at baseline in healthy milk and significantly raised in subclinical milk. The most abundant miRNAs dominated the profiles but changed little; the chosen ones were of middling abundance and variable. The logic is sound, since an electrode needs some baseline to work with. What the text does not say is which miRNAs were left out, for instance why miR-142-5p and miR-26a, used by Tzelos and colleagues, did not make the panel.

The microbiology shows how soft the label "healthy" is. Median total bacterial count was 2.43 log CFU/mL in healthy cows and 3.34 in subclinical ones, a difference of about ten-fold. One of the target pathogens (the species name is lost in the text version I read) was found in none of the five healthy cows and in all five subclinical ones. Another turned up in three of the healthy cows and one of the subclinical group. Co-isolation of two or more target pathogens occurred in three of five healthy cows and in all five subclinical cows. So the milk of a "healthy" cow is not sterile; the authors use that as a reason to favour diagnosis by host response, but the same data show the classification is noisy.

## The 0.1 nanomolar the electrode reads

The sensor design is neat and spare. A single-stranded DNA probe, thiolated at one end and tagged with methylene blue at the other, is anchored to a gold electrode. When the target miRNA pairs with it, the probe stiffens, the methylene blue moves away from the surface, and the square-wave voltammetry current changes. No reagents, no amplification.

The frequency sweep is the interesting part. The same sensor behaves as "signal on" at 2 Hz (current rises with target) and as "signal off" from about 20 Hz upward. For the DNA analogue of miR-146b, signal gain at 2 Hz reached 2,958% ± 222, with wide sensor-to-sensor scatter; at 50 Hz it settled at 70% ± 0.55. The authors chose 50 Hz, a sensible decision. The enormous gain at low frequency comes from a shrinking baseline in the denominator, an apparent amplification and not a real gain in sensitivity.

Calibration used synthetic DNA analogues and synthetic miRNA in PBS. The response was sigmoidal across 0.1 to 100 nM with a limit of detection near 0.1 nM and dissociation constants between 2 and 3.5 nM. The definition of the limit is unusual too: the concentration giving 10% of the maximum signal in the saturation region, and not the conventional three-sigma criterion. In a 20 µL drop, 0.1 nM is about two femtomoles, roughly a billion molecules; that figure is my arithmetic. The paper does not report how many nM of these miRNAs the milk extracts actually contain.

Dissociation constants for the DNA analogues and the miRNA targets agree within experimental error: 3.0 ± 0.8 nM against 2.5 ± 0.4 nM for miR-146b, 2.3 ± 0.4 nM against 3.5 ± 0.6 nM for miR-223. That justifies optimising with synthetic DNA, since RNA is costly and fragile. The paper lists reusability of the sensors as a feature; I could not find a regeneration experiment in the text.

In the selectivity test, a fully complementary target gave about 71% signal while non-complementary sequences stayed between 2% and 18%. A target with two mismatched bases gave about 46%. A two-base difference thus removes only about a third of the signal. That does not look like enough resolving power to separate miRNA family members, and closely related relatives sharing a seed sequence may be read largely as one species by this sensor. I draw this from the paper's own numbers; there is no direct test with close relatives.

The milk results have to be read inside this frame. In healthy cows signal gain was 6% for miR-146b, 7% for miR-221 and 10% for miR-223; in subclinical cows 20%, 19% and 16%. Synthetic target at 100 nM gives about 70%. The extracts sit at the bottom of the curve, which limits how finely the signal can resolve anything. Repeatability across sensors is good (RSD under 3%, n = 5), but that says sensors of one type agree on one extract, and it says nothing about variation between cows.

## Five cows against five cows

In milk, two sensors reached significance: miR-146b (p < 0.05) and miR-221. The miR-223 difference did not. Areas under the ROC curve were 0.88 for miR-221, 0.80 for miR-146b and 0.70 for miR-223. These come from ten cows. With five against five there are 25 healthy-subclinical pairs; an AUC of 0.88 means about 22 of them ordered correctly, 0.70 about 17.5. Swapping a single pair moves the AUC by 0.04. The paper names the Wilson/Brown method for the confidence intervals, but I could not find the intervals in the text.

The correlations show the same small-sample problem. SCC correlated with miR-221 at ρ = 0.83 (p = 0.015) and with miR-146b at ρ = 0.81 (p = 0.022); for miR-223 ρ = 0.71 (p = 0.058). On ten pairs, ρ = 0.83 would give a p near 0.003; the reported 0.015 looks more like seven or eight pairs. That is my rough calculation, and the paper does not say how many pairs entered. The authors also state that the sequencing and sensor work used different cows sampled in different seasons. That is their explanation for the weak miR-223 sensor: expression can move with the animal, the stage of infection and the season.

The relationships among the three miRNAs also have to be read inside the same small sample. miR-146b correlated with miR-221 at ρ = 0.85 (p = 0.003), with miR-223 at 0.68, and miR-221 with miR-223 at 0.54 (p = 0.11); the authors interpret miR-146b as a central node of regulation. Since all three rise with SCC, they are expected to rise together; a shared third variable, the severity of inflammation, can produce these correlations alone. Ten animals are too few to build a regulatory network. It is still interesting that miR-146b stayed unrelated to bacterial count while miR-221 correlated significantly with bacterial load; the authors read this as miR-146b reflecting host inflammation and not microbial burden.

I see a logical problem in the title's claim of "early diagnosis". The subclinical group is defined by SCC, 200,000 to 500,000 cells/mL, and the sensor signal tracks SCC at ρ near 0.8. This design cannot show that the sensor rises before SCC does, or catches infection that SCC misses; the reference standard is SCC itself. Tzelos and colleagues approached the same question with a larger sample and RT-qPCR. In 236 quarter milk samples, all four miRNAs climbed as the California Mastitis Test (CMT) score rose, and miR-223 and miR-142-5p picked out the first inflammatory change (CMT 1) with 100% sensitivity and more than 81% specificity (Tzelos et al., 2022). The new sensor is not there yet, but that is where it has to get.

## Whose voice is milk miR-223

The miR-223 story does not trace to a single cell type. Zhou and colleagues showed that LPS lowers bta-miR-223 in bovine mammary epithelial cells, and that miR-223 binds the 3′ UTRs of NLRP3 and Keap1 to dampen inflammation and oxidative stress (Zhou et al., 2023). Liu and colleagues reported that raising miR-223 in MAC-T cells suppressed apoptosis and necrosis caused by *Staphylococcus aureus* (Liu et al., 2025). So in epithelium infection pushes miR-223 down, and miR-223 looks protective. In milk it goes up. In chronic subclinical quarters, Saenz-de-Juano and colleagues found bta-miR-223-3p the most upregulated miRNA in milk extracellular vesicles, with the rest of the changes specific to each cow (Saenz-de-Juano et al., 2022).

A miRNA that falls in epithelium and rises in milk probably does not come from the epithelium. Dall'Olio and colleagues found miR-223 up in PHA-stimulated lymphocytes in culture and miR-26 down in LPS-stimulated neutrophils; in milk, miR-223-3p was significantly raised in the acute stage of mastitis and not in the chronic or susceptible groups (Dall'Olio et al., 2025). Together these suggest that milk miR-223 partly reads the number and stage of immune cells. It is a hypothesis; no study has separated the cellular source of milk miR-223 directly. If it holds, though, it is no surprise that the miR-223 sensor was only borderline-correlated with SCC in the new paper: it would be reading a different RNA pool from the one a cell count captures.

There are practical questions as well. The paper says "amplification-free", and that is true: no PCR. But to get RNA, 50 mL of milk is centrifuged twice and filtered, then run through an extraction kit. That is a long preparation chain for a one-step stall-side test. Another study by Saenz-de-Juano and colleagues showed that keeping raw milk at 4 °C for a week did not change vesicle size, concentration or bta-miR-223-3p levels (Saenz-de-Juano et al., 2023). That eases transport of samples; it does not remove the extraction step.

A local note. Özkan and colleagues, from the genetics and obstetrics departments of my own faculty, reported miR-27a-3p and miR-223 up and miR-125b down in 20 CMT-positive versus 20 CMT-negative milk samples, with miR-223 positively correlated with SCC (Özkan et al., 2024). Studies from different countries keep finding miR-223. That consistency makes it a dependable marker of inflammation; it does not make it a marker specific to one disease.

The authors themselves say the time dimension is missing: whether miR-146b and miR-223 rise with SCC early and then switch to negative feedback is unknown in natural mastitis. A study that tracks the sensor and a differential cell count in the same quarter over weeks could show whether the sensor is just another name for the neutrophil count.
