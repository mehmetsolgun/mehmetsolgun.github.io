Leukocytes separated on a Percoll gradient from the blood of six horses were held with ivermectin for 30 minutes, then stimulated for 2.5 hours with LPS and for one more hour with nigericin. At the end, IL-1β mRNA came out higher than in cells that had received LPS and nigericin alone. Apaoblaza and colleagues at the Universidad Austral de Chile, publishing in *Veterinary and Animal Science* in October 2026, read this as evidence that a routine antiparasitic drug can push innate immune cells toward an inflammatory transcription response (Apaoblaza et al., 2026). Every word of that sentence needs weighing, because the design, the dose and the thing actually measured limit how much the result can carry.

The setup works like this. The animals were clinically and haematologically healthy horses aged 8 to 11 years and weighing 420 to 450 kg, kept on pasture at the university teaching hospital, and each was sampled once. A mixture of mononuclear and polymorphonuclear cells, in roughly a 4:5 ratio, was seeded at about five million cells per well in six-well plates. Ivermectin went in at 2 or 4 µg/mL in 0.1% DMSO. LPS served as signal one and nigericin as signal two: LPS primes through NF-κB, switching on NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β transcription, and the second stimulus triggers assembly of the inflammasome and caspase-1 activation (Swanson et al., 2019). There were five conditions: 2 µg/mL ivermectin alone, DMSO alone, LPS plus nigericin alone, 2 µg/mL ivermectin plus the stimulus, and 4 µg/mL ivermectin plus the stimulus. Targets were read by RT-qPCR: IL-1β, IL-6, CXCL8 (IL-8), TNF-α, IL-18, and the inflammasome components NLRP3, PYCARD (ASC) and CASP1. Normalisation used a single housekeeping gene, HPRT1, with the 2^−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

## Six horses, one reference gene

Here is what came out. IL-1β rose significantly at both ivermectin doses (p < 0.05), and the rise exceeded the positive control that had received only LPS and nigericin. CXCL8 rose significantly relative to that positive control, and IL-6 followed a similar pattern. TNF-α was detected in the positive control and in ivermectin-treated stimulated cells, but the groups did not separate. IL-18 showed a trend upward at 4 µg/mL that was not significant. NLRP3 crept up slightly at 2 µg/mL without reaching significance; PYCARD and CASP1 did not change. Ivermectin at 2, 4, 8 and 12 µg/mL left cell viability, measured by MTT, untouched.

There are no coefficients in the text. The paper does not give fold changes as numbers, only as bar charts, so I cannot write down a magnitude, and that means I cannot tell a rise from a large rise. With six animals in a paired design, normality was checked with Shapiro-Wilk and the five conditions were compared with Tukey's test. Because every animal met every condition, between-horse variability shrinks, which is a real strength. Still, n = 6 is small, and the authors themselves allow that it may explain why NLRP3, IL-18 and TNF-α showed no significant difference.

The single reference gene bothers me more. Vandesompele and colleagues showed in human tissues that the habit of normalising to one control gene produces relatively large errors in a significant proportion of samples, and they proposed the geometric mean of several genes instead (Vandesompele et al., 2002). Apaoblaza and colleagues report no test of whether HPRT1 stays stable under LPS, nigericin and ivermectin. If it does, there is no problem. Because the study does not show how housekeeping genes behave in LPS-stimulated leukocytes, every fold change in a 2^−ΔΔCt calculation sits on a denominator that could have drifted. In the paper that box is empty.

The timing is also a single snapshot. Cells were collected after the last stimulus, so the measurement is a cross-section about 3.5 hours after LPS began, following a 30-minute ivermectin pre-incubation. Without kinetics, it is impossible to say whether ivermectin enlarged the response or brought it forward. The authors suggest that the slight NLRP3 rise may reflect the temporal dynamics of the response more than an effect of the drug. The RNA workflow was TRIzol, 12 hours at −20 °C, DNase treatment, reverse transcription of 2 µg RNA with M-MLV, and SYBR Green detection. In the methods section I read, there is no mention of an RNA integrity measurement or a no-reverse-transcriptase control. Such steps are often done and simply not written down, but when they are not written down a reader cannot verify them.

A few smaller inconsistencies caught my eye. The methods describe six horses and then name "four mares and one gelding", which adds up to five. The nigericin concentration is printed as 0.5 mg/mL, which reads like a unit slip. In the design table, DMSO is listed for the vehicle control and the ivermectin groups but not for the LPS-plus-nigericin positive control. A 0.1% DMSO effect on this response seems unlikely, yet the control does not match the treatment. The ivermectin-alone arm also exists only at 2 µg/mL, so the effect of 4 µg/mL by itself is unknown. None of this overturns the result on its own; together these details narrow how the result should be read.

## The mouse literature points the other way

There is a good deal of data on ivermectin suppressing inflammation, and almost all of it comes from rodents. Zhang and colleagues reported that ivermectin improved survival in mice given a lethal dose of LPS, lowered TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 production both in the animals and in RAW 264.7 macrophages, and suppressed NF-κB translocation (Zhang et al., 2008). Ma and colleagues found in mice with LPS- or bleomycin-induced acute lung injury that 1 or 2 mg/kg ivermectin lowered plasma TNF-α and IL-6 and raised IκBα expression (Ma et al., 2022). Abd-Elmawla and colleagues reported that 0.6 mg/kg ivermectin in rats with bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis reduced NLRP3 and ASC gene expression and IL-1β levels (Abd-Elmawla et al., 2023). The horse paper cites that study as a murine model; it was done in rats.

The strength of the stimulus differs as well: Zhang and colleagues used LPS at 1 µg/mL on macrophages, while the horse cells received 100 ng/mL. The equine result runs in the opposite direction from this body of work. The authors attribute it to species, dose, exposure time and cell type, and note that both anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory reports exist for ivermectin. That framing is reasonable, but one thing in the reference list stuck with me. The paper counts Ventre and colleagues among examples of context-dependent pro-inflammatory responses. The abstract of that study says the opposite: topical ivermectin reduced allergic skin inflammation in mice, along with T-cell activation and cytokine production (Ventre et al., 2017). I could not check from the full text whether the citation serves a different purpose, but at abstract level it does not fit. The "dual profile" of ivermectin is less well supported in the paper than it looks on the page.

## An NLRP3 that may not need potassium

LPS plus nigericin is a widely used model for driving the NLRP3 inflammasome. In horses, though, the mechanism the model rests on is not on firm ground. Ahn and colleagues found that ATP, nigericin, aluminium crystals and monosodium urate crystals raised IL-1β secretion in a dose-dependent way in equine peripheral blood mononuclear cells, but that blocking potassium efflux did not completely abolish that secretion (Ahn et al., 2020). The equine NLRP3 may depend on potassium efflux less than it does in other species. Apaoblaza and colleagues mention this in the discussion. What nags me is that the agent chosen as the second signal may not be as clean an inflammasome trigger in this species as the model assumes. And the readout is transcript. Inflammasome activity shows up in caspase-1 activity and mature IL-1β protein. Unchanged NLRP3, PYCARD and CASP1 mRNA does not show that the inflammasome was unaffected, and the authors write in the abstract that it should not be read that way.

The concluding paragraph of the paper is bolder than its abstract. The abstract labels the inflammasome result as secondary and exploratory and says it should not be read as evidence that the canonical pathway is uninvolved. The final paragraph says the rise in cytokine transcripts may occur, at least in part, through mechanisms not dependent on transcriptional upregulation of the canonical inflammasome pathway, and then adds at once that inflammasome activation is regulated mainly after translation, so functional assays are needed. The two statements do not contradict each other, but their emphasis differs. I find the abstract's caution the more accurate of the two.

Vinther and colleagues, working with six horses in an LPS synovitis and endotoxaemia model, showed that RT-qPCR expression of 22 inflammation-related genes in blood leukocytes could tell local inflammation from systemic inflammation (Vinther et al., 2016). So there is precedent for transcript profiling being informative in horses. That was a live-animal model, though. Here the cells are stimulated in a tube, and Percoll separation can prime polymorphonuclear cells on its own (a limitation the authors raise themselves), which could shift baseline transcription.

## The face IL-1β wears in the stable

In equine medicine this cytokine is no abstract readout. In the study by Colbath and colleagues, 12 horses received a single intra-articular injection of just 75 ng of recombinant IL-1β into the middle carpal or tibiotarsal joint, producing clinical and cytological synovitis that could last beyond a week, and the same dose produced different responses in the two joints (Colbath et al., 2018). The synovitis model is built directly on this molecule. In an earlier study from the same Chilean group, six asthmatic horses in clinical remission were split by body condition; the obese ones had higher serum IL-1β and neutrophil counts, and their neutrophils showed higher IL-1β gene expression in response to LPS (Albornoz et al., 2025). Both studies tie a disease phenotype to IL-1β. At first glance that makes the amplification of the LPS response by ivermectin look clinically meaningful. But those studies measured disease and a joint model; they did not measure ivermectin. Building the bridge would require stimulating, ex vivo, blood taken from horses that have actually received the drug.

## Thirty-three to a hundred and thirty-three times

Of all the numbers, this one affected me most. The authors write in the discussion that peak plasma concentrations after standard therapeutic dosing in horses are reported at 30 to 60 ng/mL, and that the 2 to 4 µg/mL they used sits one to two orders of magnitude above that. I did the arithmetic myself: 2,000 ng/mL over 60 ng/mL is about 33, and 4,000 ng/mL over 30 ng/mL is about 133. The cells were exposed to thirty-three to a hundred and thirty-three times the level seen in a horse's blood. The dose was chosen from earlier cell studies to maximise the chance of seeing a gene-expression effect in a short exposure; that is a scientific choice, but it does not allow the result to transfer straight to the clinic. The MTT assay showed viability holding up to 12 µg/mL, so these doses do not kill the cells, but they do not occur in the horse either.

With a dose gap this wide, the obvious question is why ivermectin dampens inflammation in mouse studies and amplifies it in horse leukocytes. Cell mixture, species, duration and dose all change at once, and the study cannot say which is responsible. As the authors note, neutrophil inflammasome regulation rests on distinct and less well-characterised mechanisms, and in a mixed preparation we cannot see which cells raised the cytokine mRNA. They propose purified monocyte experiments as the next step.

The experiment that would answer the question is easy to describe. Purify mononuclear cells and neutrophils separately; run ivermectin as a dose series reaching down to 30 to 60 ng/mL, near plasma levels, and up to 4 µg/mL; measure IL-1β protein by ELISA and caspase-1 activity with a functional assay; use several reference genes; and put the same DMSO in every group. The cells of six horses might be enough for this, since the paired design already adds power.

Ivermectin is a widely used antiparasitic in horses, and the authors point out that its effects on immune cells have been studied very little in the species; this paper is among the first steps into that gap. Whether anything is left of the IL-1β signal at a real plasma level of 0.03 to 0.06 µg/mL, after the same 30-minute exposure, is a question nobody has asked yet.
