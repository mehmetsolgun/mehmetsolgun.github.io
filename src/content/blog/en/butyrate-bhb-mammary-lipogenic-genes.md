After 24 hours in 12 millimolar sodium butyrate, bovine mammary epithelial cells (MAC-T) carried 25 times more SREBF1c mRNA than their untreated neighbours. That is the biggest number in a paper by Haile and Harvatine of Penn State, published in *JDS Communications* in August 2026. A smaller number from a different salt caught my attention more. Sodium β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB) at 8 millimolar raised FASN expression roughly 16-fold in the same cells while leaving SREBF1c, one of the master transcription factors of lipogenic enzymes, exactly where it was (Haile and Harvatine, 2026).

The design is plain. MAC-T cells (passages 14 and 15) were grown to about 80% confluence in six-well plates, starved of serum for 18 hours, and then exposed for 24 hours to 0, 4, 8 or 12 mM sodium acetate, butyrate or BHB in medium with neither serum nor insulin. Six transcripts were read out by RT-qPCR: ACSS2, ACACA, FASN, SCD1, INSIG1 and SREBF1c, with three biological replicates per dose. The authors removed insulin and serum on purpose, reasoning that if the lipogenic enzymes were already fully switched on, a short-chain fatty acid would have no room to push them further. I will come back to that choice, because it shapes how far the results can be carried.

## Three salts, three gene programs

Milk fat is built from rumen fermentation products, and acetate sits at the top of that list. Urrutia and Harvatine infused 5, 10 and 15 mol of acetate per day into the rumens of six lactating cows; milk fat yield rose by 7%, 16% and 14%, mostly through 16-carbon fatty acids (Urrutia and Harvatine, 2017). The same infusion pushed plasma BHB up by 29% to 78% after feeding, so the cow experiment cannot say which molecule did what inside the udder. Haile and Harvatine try to separate them in a dish: what does a mammary epithelial cell do with each of these salts when the rest of the body is out of the picture?

Acetate gave the quietest answer. ACSS2, the gene for the enzyme that turns acetate into acetyl-CoA, rose about 1.6-fold at 8 mM, and SCD1 about 2-fold at 8 and 12 mM. FASN, ACACA and INSIG1 did not move. SREBF1c crept up numerically at 4 mM, fell back at 12 mM relative to 4 mM, and never separated from control.

The modest ACSS2 rise, in the authors' reading, may point to acetate being used directly as a carbon source; the gene for the enzyme that converts acetate to acetyl-CoA going up fits that story. The fact that FASN did not move suggests to them that acetate may promote fat synthesis through post-translational or signalling routes more than through strong transcriptional activation. The interpretation is plausible but untested, because this study measured neither protein nor signalling pathways.

Butyrate pulled on everything at once. ACSS2 was up 3.9-fold at 4 mM and more than 12-fold at 12 mM. FASN reached about 3.3-fold and ACACA about 3-fold, SCD1 rose roughly 3.5-fold at every dose, and INSIG1 climbed to about 5.5-fold at 8 mM and 6.2-fold at 12 mM. SREBF1c did nothing at 4 mM, jumped 16.8-fold at 8 mM and 25-fold at 12 mM. Nothing at four, an explosion at eight: that looks like a threshold. The authors attribute the response to two properties of butyrate. It is the preferred primer for fatty acid synthesis through butyryl-CoA, and it inhibits histone deacetylases (HDACs). The HDAC part was not measured here; histone acetylation was not examined, so the interpretation leans on other laboratories' data. Chriett and colleagues reported that butyrate is a strong HDAC inhibitor in rat myotubes and endothelial cells while they could not detect the same activity for BHB (Chriett et al., 2019). Different species and different cell types, but the direction fits.

An older goat study complicates the picture a little. Sun and colleagues exposed goat mammary epithelial cells to 3 mM acetate, propionate or butyrate for 24 hours. Acetate raised SCD1 and FABP3, which matches the SCD1 rise in MAC-T. Butyrate raised SREBP1 and SCD1 but lowered FASN mRNA (Sun et al., 2016). In Haile and Harvatine's cells butyrate raised FASN 3.3-fold. Same salt, similar duration, a related species, opposite sign for FASN. The discussion of the new paper stresses consistency between the goat and bovine models; I did not see it address this reversal.

The doses show a common pattern. For most genes the response peaked between 4 and 8 mM and flattened or fell back at 12 mM. With butyrate, FASN and ACACA did not rise between 8 and 12 mM, and with BHB, FASN did not climb past 8 mM. The authors read this as a sign that concentration determines the transcriptional outcome. I cannot rule out another reading: a plateau might mark the ceiling of what the cells can do, or a side effect of the high dose on the medium. The study did not quantify cell viability; only for acetate does the text say that no cytotoxicity was visible at any concentration, and there is no equivalent sentence for butyrate or BHB. Medium pH was measured and did not differ from control. I found no osmolarity or sodium control, for instance an equal amount of sodium chloride. A 12 mM sodium salt adds 12 mM sodium to the medium and roughly 24 mOsm/kg of osmotic load (my arithmetic). I do not know whether that load by itself provokes a gene response, though the fact that acetate did almost nothing under the same load suggests it is not the main explanation.

## The experiment where BHB drove FASN alone

The authors expected a limited response to BHB and got one, but not in the shape they expected. ACSS2 and SCD1 did not change. FASN rose about 8-fold at 4 mM and about 16-fold at 8 mM, with no further gain at 12 mM. ACACA went down at 4 and 12 mM. SREBF1c and INSIG1 were unaffected (P > 0.30). PPARG and FABP3 went up with BHB (P < 0.01), and neither acetate nor butyrate touched them.

The SREBP1 axis is treated as the center of mammary lipid synthesis. The review by Harvatine, Boisclair and Bauman collects the evidence that key lipogenic enzymes are transcriptionally regulated through SREBP1 (Harvatine et al., 2009). Against that background, a 16-fold FASN rise with a flat SREBF1c says that even at the mRNA level there is no single switch. The authors list threshold effects, cell-specific BHB metabolism and factors other than SREBF1 as possible explanations. A reasonable list. I would go one step further. ACACA fell at the same doses at which FASN soared, and that stops me from reading the FASN increase as lipogenic activation. The two enzymes work one after the other on the same pathway. If one rises sixteen-fold while the other drops, the question of whether the mRNAs reflect the cell's real fat-making capacity stays open. The study did not measure triglyceride, lipid droplets or enzyme protein, and the authors say so themselves.

Others have asked the question with cell outputs alongside the transcripts. Ali and colleagues reported that acetate, BHB and their combination raised lipogenic gene mRNA in dairy cow mammary epithelial cells, together with ACACA, FASN and SCD1 protein and SREBP1, and that cytosolic triglyceride content went up as well (Ali et al., 2021). In Haile and Harvatine's cells BHB did not raise ACACA and actually lowered it at two doses, and acetate left both FASN and ACACA alone. Zhao and colleagues showed in bovine mammary epithelial cells that acetate between 0 and 12 mM raised FASN, ACACA and SREBP1 in a dose-dependent way through the mTOR/eIF4E pathway; 6 mM was the dose used in their follow-up experiments (Zhao et al., 2021). Qi and colleagues tied acetate to the same direction through GPR41/GPR43 and mTORC1 in mouse HC11 cells and in mice (Qi et al., 2023). Set beside those three studies, the silence of FASN under acetate in MAC-T stands out. Where does the difference come from? I cannot reconstruct the medium conditions of Ali and colleagues from the abstract. Haile and Harvatine put it down to dose and context thresholds.

## What the cell saw and what the cow sees

In the paper's own introduction, arterial butyrate in well-fed cows past peak lactation sits around 0.01 to 0.03 mmol/L, while BHB runs at 0.5 to 0.8 mmol/L. Butyrate is largely converted to BHB in the rumen wall and the liver, so it is nearly absent from circulation. The 4 to 12 mM applied to the cells equals somewhat over a hundred times to over a thousand times those blood levels for butyrate, and five to twenty-four times for BHB (my own arithmetic; the paper says only that the doses are much higher than plasma concentrations). The authors state this openly and suggest testing doses below 4 mM.

In the cow, the assumption that butyrate raises milk fat is still shaky. In the 12-cow, 3 × 3 Latin square trial by Urrutia and colleagues, sodium acetate at 2.9% of diet dry matter raised milk fat yield by 90 g/d. Calcium butyrate at 2.5%, a carbon-equivalent dose, cut dry matter intake by 2.6 kg/d, milk yield by 1.65 kg/d and milk fat yield by 60 g/d, and it raised milk trans-10 C18:1 by 18% (Urrutia et al., 2019). Doses and forms differ, so I would not count this as a refutation of the MAC-T result. It does remind me that a molecule producing a 25-fold SREBF1c response in a dish can lower milk fat when fed. Butyrate appears to have shifted rumen biohydrogenation in that trial; what reaches the gland may not be the salt that went into the feed bunk.

The serum and insulin starvation deserves its own mention. Lowering the baseline on purpose makes the salts' effect visible, but a real mammary gland never works in that hormonal environment. Expression was measured once, at 24 hours, so early and late responses are not separated.

A note on normalisation, because in RT-qPCR data that is often where the result is decided. Target genes were divided by a normalisation factor computed as the geometric mean of ACTB, B2M and 18S rRNA, scaled to the geometric mean across all samples. Three reference genes is good practice. I could not find an analysis in the methods showing that these three stay stable under butyrate, acetate and BHB. If they do, nothing is wrong. If they do not, every fold change carries some drift in the denominator, and the text I have does not settle that.

As for the statistics, the dose effect was tested separately for each salt with a general linear model, dose as the fixed effect and each compound within itself. A sentence like "butyrate is far stronger than acetate" therefore comes from setting two separate dose-response analyses side by side, not from a direct comparison. The gap is large enough that this changes little for most genes. Even so, with three replicates per dose I would be wary of reading much into small differences between neighbouring doses.

The authors' logic is clear. Because mammary lipogenesis is regulated largely at the level of gene expression, these changes would be expected to raise milk fat, and an intervention that moves many enzymes at once should do more than one that moves few, depending on which step is limiting. That last condition carries everything. If BHB moves only FASN and FASN is the limiting step in the cell, a single gene is enough. If the limiting step is ACACA, or the supply of a fatty acid primer, then raising FASN sixteen-fold changes nothing. A dish of cells cannot answer that; an experiment that measures flux can. MAC-T is also an immortalised cell line and does not carry the hormonal and cellular diversity of lactating mammary tissue, a limit the authors acknowledge themselves.

One experiment would complete this dataset: measure FASN protein and triglyceride content in BHB-treated MAC-T cells. If FASN protein also rises sixteen-fold and the drop in ACACA does not cap cellular output, then the share of BHB, which circulates in the cow at 0.5 to 0.8 mmol/L, in milk fat synthesis needs to be recalculated. If triglyceride does not budge, the 16-fold FASN rise will remain an increase that left SREBF1c untouched, and this paper will not tell us what it meant.
