When GPR3 was silenced in goat mammary epithelial cells, three classes of lipid fell together: triglycerides, diglycerides and a group of branched fatty acid esters known as FAHFAs. That sentence in the abstract of Fan and colleagues' October 2026 paper in the *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry* is what caught my attention (Fan et al., 2026). The headline claim is a different one: glycitin, an isoflavone from soy, increases milk fat synthesis in these cells, and it may do so through an orphan receptor called GPR3.

I could not get the full text. The paper is not open access, and all I have is the published abstract. So I cannot say anything about doses, durations, numbers of replicates or statistics, and I will not pretend otherwise. What follows reports what the abstract says and nothing it does not say. Instead I want to place the study next to several papers published over the past year or so, because a story that looks tidy on its own tends to look less tidy once its neighbours are read with it.

## Three lipid classes drop together

According to the abstract, glycitin increased triglyceride accumulation, lipid droplet formation and lipogenic gene expression in goat mammary epithelial cells (GMECs). Docking and molecular dynamics simulations suggested a plausible interaction between glycitin and GPR3. The rise in cyclic AMP (cAMP) was weakened when GPR3 was knocked down. Glycitin also raised GPR3 expression and reduced transcriptional repression associated with MYC-associated factor X (MAX). Knocking down GPR3 reduced triglycerides, diacylglycerols and FAHFAs.

The authors' own closing sentence is careful: GPR3-associated signalling is "a potential mechanism" linking glycitin to milk fat synthesis. I stop at the same place. Docking and simulation are computational; they say that a ligand could sit in a receptor, not that it does. A direct binding measurement does not appear in the abstract.

"Reduced MAX-associated repression" is a single clause. It does not say which genes were released from repression, or what carries the signal between the receptor and the transcription machinery. The full text may have a scheme for that. I cannot invent one to fill the gap.

What I would look for if I get hold of the full text is easy to list. Which lipogenic genes were measured, and whether the change appears only in mRNA or in protein too. How efficient the knockdown was, and whether it was compared with a scrambled siRNA control. What triglyceride did in GPR3-silenced cells without glycitin: if the baseline is already low, a knockdown that abolishes the glycitin effect cannot be told apart from a cell that simply makes less fat. Whether there is a dose-response curve, whether cell viability was checked, and how many independent goat cell isolates the experiment was repeated with. How the reference gene for RT-qPCR was chosen is no small matter either, because some reference genes drift with culture conditions. None of this can be read out of an abstract.

## The orphan receptor's ligand problem

To see why GPR3 still counts as an orphan, it is enough to read Pyne and colleagues' 2026 study. GPR3 is a constitutively active G protein-coupled receptor, which means it produces cAMP without any ligand at all; it is known to regulate β-amyloid deposition in the brain and metabolism in brown adipose tissue. Many compounds have been proposed to activate it, yet the receptor is still called an orphan. Structural work revealed a hydrophobic tunnel in GPR3, which raised the idea that an endogenous agonist might be a lipid. The authors tested the reported agonists one by one. Only the synthetic diphenyleneiodonium chloride and the endogenous lipids oleoylethanolamide (OEA) and oleamide stimulated cAMP generation in a GPR3-dependent way. OEA did not behave like a classical agonist: activation crept up over several hours, and chronic OEA raised the amount of GPR3 on the cell surface. The authors propose that OEA acts as a pharmacological chaperone, helping the receptor travel from the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi to the plasma membrane (Pyne et al., 2026).

That raises two questions about the glycitin result. The first is how a molecule expected to sit in a hydrophobic tunnel relates to glycitin, which is an isoflavone glycoside: it carries a sugar and is not a lipid. That does not mean it cannot bind. In a field where most proposed ligands failed the test, though, a docking calculation alone is thin support. The second is that glycitin raised GPR3 expression. More of a constitutively active receptor can mean more cAMP even when no ligand ever binds, and the chaperone mechanism proposed for OEA points in the same direction, with a compound increasing the amount of receptor. The abstract does not distinguish between these scenarios for glycitin. That is my question, not the authors' claim. A knockdown would blunt the cAMP response under either one, so it would give the same result.

A loop that Chan and colleagues described in brown adipose tissue makes the question more interesting. Cold-induced serum amyloid A3 (SAA3) amplifies thermogenic signalling through a GPR3-dependent cAMP/PKA axis, and this activation upregulates the transcription of both Saa3 and Gpr3, building an autocrine and paracrine amplification loop (Chan et al., 2026). If a similar loop exists in mammary cells, the rise in GPR3 expression after glycitin could be a consequence of the cAMP increase and not its cause. That is again my reading; the abstract does not show the order of events. Tissue context matters too: the same receptor drives an energy-burning pathway in brown fat and may be driving fat storage in mammary cells. A shared receptor does not guarantee shared downstream wiring.

## A receptor that pulls the other way

Leave the GPR3 paper alone and it looks simple. Next to its neighbours the picture gets more complicated. Zhang and colleagues tried serotonin on the same cell type. At 100 µM it suppressed the mRNA of ACC, FASN, SREBP1, SCD1 and ELOVL6, lowered triglyceride and total cholesterol, reduced lipid droplets and raised intracellular calcium, and the effect was carried by the receptor HTR2A. Sarpogrelate, a specific HTR2A antagonist, did the reverse: triglycerides, cholesterol and droplet accumulation went up (Zhang et al., 2025). In one cell type, one receptor switches lipid synthesis on and another switches it off. And 100 µM is a high concentration; I cannot tell from the abstract what its physiological counterpart would be. There is nothing scandalous about it. It means that a cell has no single milk fat button, and that any glycitin effect should be read together with the question of which receptors were stimulated at the same moment. GPR3 may be only one of them.

The number of layers at which fat synthesis is tuned shows up in other work as well. Xiong and colleagues compared mammary tissue of Saanen goats at peak lactation and in the dry period: 862 significantly acetylated proteins, 2,028 acetylation sites, and 54 candidates with possible roles in milk protein, milk fat and lactose synthesis. Acetylation of BDH1 increased lipid droplet formation and triglyceride synthesis in GMECs and pushed up LXRα, ACSL1 and SCD1; deacetylation lowered SCD1, FASN and ACSL1. The enzymes that remove the acetyl group appeared to belong to the HDAC family (Xiong et al., 2025). In the SIRT7 study of Li and colleagues, an inhibitor at 15 µmol/L increased lipid droplet and triglyceride content; by GC-MS, C16:0, C15:0, C20:0, C24:0, C16:1 and C18:1n9c went up, while C11:0 and EPA fell. The authors call their findings preliminary evidence (Li et al., 2026). A shift toward palmitate also leaves open which direction would count as better for milk quality.

## A droplet in a dish is not milk in a pail

These four cell studies share more than the goat and the mammary cell. They share a readout, which is the triglyceride and lipid droplet content inside the cell. The fat that reaches a milking pail is not a direct equivalent. Formation of the milk fat globule, secretion across the apical membrane and passage into milk are separate steps. A stimulus that makes cultured cells fill up with droplets might not raise milk fat percentage in a living animal, and if droplets stay in the cell without being secreted, the effect could even run the other way. Nothing in the abstract refutes that and nothing in it confirms it.

The design of the Xiong group is instructive here. They first compared mammary tissue of live goats at peak lactation and in the dry period, picked candidate proteins from the tissue, and then tested one of them in cells. The abstract of the glycitin study speaks only of GMECs; no lead from the animal and no tissue-level confirmation is mentioned. That does not make the study wrong. It shows where the hypothesis stands: in the dish, ahead of the animal.

In animals, a similar direction has been seen, though with a very different intervention. D'Alessandro and colleagues fed 24 lactating goats (12 per group) a polyphenol-containing silage made from grape pomace, wheat straw, cheese whey and molasses for 28 days; milk fat concentration and milk antioxidant capacity rose in the silage group, and plasma total and HDL cholesterol fell (D'Alessandro et al., 2026). That is a mixture, not an isoflavone. It shows that a polyphenol-rich diet can move milk fat in goats; it does not show that glycitin would. In the same study, meat from three male kids per group had higher polyunsaturated and lower monounsaturated fatty acids, so dietary polyphenols seem to have moved the fatty acid profile as well, although a subsample of three animals does not support much more than that. Glycitin has its own dose question: the abstract speaks of "dietary isoflavones", but how much glycitin would pass through the rumen and reach mammary tissue, relative to the concentration used in culture, is not in the abstract. Rumen microbes are known to transform many plant compounds, and what that means for glycitin cannot be said from this data.

## FAHFAs in the shadow of lipogenesis

The sentence in the abstract that struck me as strangest is the one about FAHFAs. These are the branched fatty acid esters of hydroxy fatty acids that Yore and colleagues described in *Cell* in 2014. The first finding came from adipose tissue: in mice that overexpress Glut4 in adipocytes and have high lipogenesis, these lipids were elevated 16- to 18-fold; PAHSA levels correlated with insulin sensitivity, and in adipocytes they signalled through GPR120 (Yore et al., 2014). In mice, PAHSA administration lowered glycaemia, improved glucose tolerance, stimulated GLP-1 and insulin secretion and reduced adipose tissue inflammation. For a FAHFA in milk to become a functional food argument one day, both the level in milk and an effect after oral intake would have to be shown separately; as far as I could find, neither exists yet for goat milk.

What happens in a cow was tested by Arif and colleagues, who followed 12 multiparous Holstein cows in a 4 × 4 Latin square. When palmitic or stearic acid supplements were given at 1.95% of dietary dry matter, plasma 9-PAHPA rose 2.9-fold with palmitic acid and 9-SAHSA rose 2.7-fold with stearic acid. In milk, eight non-esterified FAHFAs were quantified, but only 12-PAHSA increased, with stearic acid; the authors report limited effects of the supplements on milk fat FAHFAs and say that the functional roles of these lipids need further exploration. The same abstract notes that the major FAHFAs are synthesised from palmitic, stearic and oleic acid (Arif et al., 2025). That the plasma rise barely showed up in milk also weakens any assumption that a FAHFA change measured in cells would carry over into milk.

With both facts on the table, the GPR3 result reads differently. FAHFAs were raised in a high-lipogenesis mouse model, and their raw materials are palmitate and stearate. If knocking down GPR3 makes cells build less triglyceride, the FAHFA drop need not be an effect of GPR3 on FAHFA synthesis; it could be the shadow of a general fall in fatty acid flux. This is my interpretation, and the abstract does not separate the two possibilities. Separating them would need an analysis that expresses FAHFA levels relative to the total fatty acid pool.

How much FAHFA goat milk contains, whether a feed additive could move it, and whether any shift would carry health meaning are open questions. The most concrete question left is a much plainer one: if a goat is fed glycitin, does milk fat actually go up, and in mammary tissue where GPR3 has been silenced, does that rise disappear?
