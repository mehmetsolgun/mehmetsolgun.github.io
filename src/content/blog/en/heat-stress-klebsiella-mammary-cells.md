After six hours of Klebsiella pneumoniae infection, 59.6% of bovine mammary epithelial cells were still alive. Warm the same cells to 42 °C before the infection and the figure falls to 20.4%. Guo and colleagues, writing in *Veterinary Quarterly* in October 2026, set out to rebuild in a culture dish something every dairy farmer sees in summer: environmental mastitis that climbs with the heat.

The farm facts behind the paper are familiar to anyone who has worked a dairy through August. In hot, humid weather the environmental load of Klebsiella rises, and clinical cases and bulk tank somatic cell counts peak in summer. Heat stress raises cortisol, which dampens lymphocyte and neutrophil function, and it weakens the blood–milk barrier. What happens when the two pressures coincide has been studied far less. The review by Zadoks and colleagues (2011) lists Klebsiella pneumoniae among the main mastitis pathogens that also occur in humans, either as commensals or as pathogens, so the organism has a foot in human medicine too.

The bacterium's way of life on the farm is part of the problem. The authors recall that it can persist in bedding, faeces, teat skin and milking liners, which lets it spread across a whole herd. Earlier work had already shown that Klebsiella isolates from mastitis can adhere to and invade mammary epithelial cells, push up cytokine and chemokine production, and disrupt mitochondria in a way that ends in ferroptosis. The picture is of an organism in direct contact with the epithelium and not only of an opportunist waiting outside it. Heat stress, the introduction adds, is felt most in high-yielding cows, where it damages mammary epithelial function and raises milk somatic cell counts.

## Twenty percent after six hours

The experimental material came from a single donor. The authors isolated primary bovine mammary epithelial cells (BMEC) from the udder tissue of one four-year-old, mid-lactation Holstein cow at the Yangzhou University experimental farm and grew them in lactogenic medium with prolactin, hydrocortisone and insulin. The infecting strain, KPTP11, is a hypermucoviscous isolate of sequence type ST35 taken from mastitic milk, applied at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. It is also a single strain, and nothing in the experiment tests whether the response seen with a hypermucoviscous ST35 isolate would hold for other Klebsiella strains.

There were four groups. Controls stayed at 37 °C for six hours. The heat stress (HS) group sat at 42 °C for six hours. The bacterial group spent two hours at 37 °C and was then infected for four. The combined group was pre-warmed at 42 °C for two hours and then infected for four hours at 42 °C. The team had first tried a six-hour infection, but the combined group lost so many cells that too few survived for downstream assays, so they moved to the four-hour model. By trypan blue, viability at four hours was 90.3% in the bacterial group and 72.1% in the combined group. At six hours the two values were 59.6% and 20.4%. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release and propidium iodide–positive cell counts were also clearly higher in the combined group than with bacteria alone. The heat shock proteins HSP27, HSP70 and HSP90 rose with heat. Heat on its own did its damage gradually: with longer heating, viability slid and LDH release climbed. For HSP70 there was even a statistical interaction with the bacteria, which means that the two stimuli produced a response that was bigger than the sum of the parts.

Two comments on the design, both of which the authors raise themselves. First, continuous heating at 42 °C is a crude imitation of a cow's summer day, since a real animal's heat load can fluctuate during the day, and the authors suggest recovery-based models for the future. Second, all findings come from one cow. Three biological replicates correspond to three independent experiments with cells from the same animal, not to cultures from three different cows, so the direction of the results cannot be said to hold for other animals on this evidence. The use of four separate viability read-outs is a point in the design's favour: CCK-8 reflects metabolic activity, trypan blue membrane integrity, LDH leaked enzyme, and calcein-AM with propidium iodide counts live and dead cells separately. When all four point the same way it becomes easier to accept that the cells are really dying.

## Flame and oxygen

The inflammatory pathway changed more than anything else. Liu and colleagues (2017) describe how the transcription factor NF-κB switches on cytokine and chemokine genes and takes part in regulating the inflammasome. The data of Guo and colleagues fit that scheme. Bacteria alone raised phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα and the levels of IL-1β and TNF-α, and p65 moved into the nucleus. With heat added, phosphorylation climbed further and nuclear p65 fluorescence rose. The interaction between the two stimuli was statistically significant. The statistical design helps here, because the two-way ANOVA tests the interaction explicitly. A significant interaction means the combined effect exceeds the sum of the two single effects. Heat adds to what the bacteria do and also multiplies the response to them. The word "synergistic" in the paper rests on that analysis.

Chemokine and antimicrobial peptide genes were up too, and a single anti-inflammatory cytokine was down. In the discussion the authors cite CXCL2 and CXCL3 as signals that call in neutrophils, and CCL2 and CCL5 as signals for monocytes and macrophages. The rise of antimicrobial peptide genes with heat is, I think, the least discussed part of this data set. The epithelial cell does not pull back its defences in the heat. It raises them, and still dies faster.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were highest in the combined group. The authors conclude that excess ROS may have facilitated NF-κB activation. This calls for caution. Morgan and Liu (2011) explain that the relation between ROS and NF-κB runs both ways: NF-κB target genes regulate the amount of ROS in the cell, while ROS can play both inhibitory and stimulatory roles in NF-κB signalling. The paper has no ROS scavenger and no NF-κB inhibitor experiment. We can see that two things rise together. We cannot see which one drives the other, and writing ROS as the cause and NF-κB as the effect hands the reader a tidier story than the data allow. A second gap appears in the discussion, where reduced antioxidant capacity is said to accompany the ROS rise, but I could not find an antioxidant measurement such as superoxide dismutase or glutathione in the methods or results. The only ROS data are the mean fluorescence of DCFH-DA read by flow cytometry, a general oxidation indicator that cannot say which species rose or where it came from.

The cytokine and chemokine rise can also be read as good news. An epithelial cell that calls in neutrophils is a cell mounting a defence. The authors call it a double-edged response, arguing that overproduction may feed cytotoxicity and tissue damage. That reading is reasonable, but the experiment cannot separate damage from defence.

## The barrier has the mRNA but not the protein

The tight junction proteins ZO-1, occludin, claudin-1 and claudin-3 gave an interesting result. Heat or bacteria alone raised the mRNA of some tight junction genes. Even so, protein was lower than in controls in the bacterial group and lowest in the combined group. In immunofluorescence images ZO-1 and occludin had withdrawn from the junctions between cells and looked diffuse inside them. The gene is on but the protein is not in place.

The authors read this as a compensatory transcriptional response: the cell senses damage to its barrier and makes more mRNA, yet protein level also depends on translation efficiency, stability, trafficking and degradation. The explanation is plausible, but the data do not prove it, since no experiment measures protein half-life or proteasomal breakdown. What strikes me is how different this picture is from what RT-qPCR could deliver on its own. If only mRNA had been measured, one could have concluded wrongly that the barrier strengthens in the heat. The result emerged because the cells were measured in two ways, abundance by western blot and location by immunofluorescence, and both point the same way, which lowers the chance that one bad antibody or one bad stain is behind it.

The discussion proposes that barrier loss comes from IL-1β and TNF-α acting through MLCK. The authors build that link on earlier studies, and MLCK was not measured in their own experiments. Testing the chain would need an experiment that neutralises the cytokines or blocks MLCK. For now we have co-occurrence: where inflammation rises, the barrier fails, and the direction of that link, as with ROS and NF-κB, remains unclear. The reverse direction, a damaged barrier feeding inflammation, is at least as plausible, since bacteria and bacterial products reach the cell interior more easily through a broken barrier.

## From making fat to burning it

For milk fat, the result is heavy. Intracellular triglyceride fell under heat and under bacteria, and was lowest in the combined group. Lipid droplet area and number fell in the same pattern in Oil Red O and BODIPY stains. The detail that caught my eye is ATP, which went up in the heat and bacteria groups compared with controls and in the combined group was no different from control. Energy level held while fat dropped, which hints that the cells were running on some other fuel, though no experiment confirms it.

At protein level FASN, ACC, SREBP1 and PPAR-γ went down. DGAT1 and PLIN2, linked to triglyceride synthesis and droplet formation, dropped as well. Against them CPT1A, the key enzyme of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, went up. Heat alone suppressed FASN, ACC and ATGL and raised CPT1A. The authors read this as a shift of resources from anabolic lipid synthesis to stress response and energy production.

The gene expression table is messier than that reading suggests. Heat alone had raised the mRNA of some genes tied to triglyceride synthesis and fatty acid uptake, yet the cell's triglyceride fell. As with the tight junctions, mRNA, protein and phenotype do not tell one story here. The team did not trust mRNA alone and also ran western blots, which was wise, since transcript levels by themselves might have led to the wrong conclusion that milk fat synthesis rises in the heat.

Baumgard and Rhoads (2013) tell a different story at the whole-animal level. Heat-stressed animals show a striking lack of fat mobilisation from adipose tissue and a reduced response to lipolytic stimuli, so the store stays closed despite an energy shortfall. They also showed that heat stress alters metabolism independently of reduced feed intake. A mammary epithelial cell is not adipose tissue, but putting the oxidation trend seen in the dish next to the absent mobilisation in the animal gives the impression of two systems spending the same resource in opposite directions. I note it as a hypothesis, and this study's data are not enough to carry it. The fall of the droplet surface protein PLIN2 in the combined group shows that the droplet itself fails to mature. For milk fat to form in the cell, fatty acid synthesis is needed, and so is packing that fat into a droplet and holding it there. This study sees a break at both links.

One more caveat on the lipid result. Intracellular triglyceride and droplet content stand in for milk fat, since the study measures neither secreted fat nor the fatty acid composition of the medium, and cultured mammary cells model the cell, not the udder. The authors themselves add that systemic factors, including lactogenic hormones, glucocorticoids and insulin signalling, are absent from this model and still need to be studied.

The message for the farm is stated openly by the authors: reducing heat stress should be part of environmental mastitis control in summer. Culture data alone cannot support that, but its direction is consistent. A more concrete question stays with me. Would the same experiment give the same result if cells were taken out of 42 °C, rested at 37 °C and then challenged with the bacterium? A real cow's heat load does not stay constant. Whether the cell remembers the heat, and how much the quiet moments between hot ones reduce the damage, could determine the timing of what a farm should do.
