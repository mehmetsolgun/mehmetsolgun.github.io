Seventeen mares, one placenta each at term, and two pieces cut from every placenta: the horn where the foal grew and the horn that stayed empty. In the study by Dos Santos and colleagues at the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine in Lisbon, published in *Research in Veterinary Science*, three genes (MMP2, TIMP1 and TIMP2) were transcribed more in the pregnant horn than in the empty one. The higher transcript is reported together with hypomethylation of the promoter regions. It reads like a tidy story, "methylation dropped, gene switched on". Then the authors measured the proteins and found no difference between the horns.

## Two horns of one placenta

The mare's uterus has two horns, and in pregnancy the foal develops mostly in one of them while the placenta reaches into both. The authors compared the pregnant horn (PH) with the non-pregnant horn (NPH) using samples from the same animals, so each mare serves as her own control. That keeps genetic and physiological differences between mares out of the comparison.

The genes fall into two sets. The first set regulates collagen turnover: the matrix metalloproteinases MMP2 and MMP9 and their tissue inhibitors TIMP1 and TIMP2. The second set steers blood vessel formation: VEGFA and its receptors VEGFR1 and VEGFR2. Transcripts were quantified by qPCR, proteins by western blot, protein location in the tissue by immunohistochemistry, and methylation of promoter CpG islands by bisulfite pyrosequencing. The same question was therefore asked at four different layers.

The first results go the expected way. MMP2, TIMP1 and TIMP2 transcripts are higher in the PH. MMP9, VEGFA, VEGFR1 and VEGFR2 do not differ between horns. What I find interesting is that the difference appears in three functionally linked genes at once rather than in one. MMP-2 takes part in breaking down basement membrane and collagen; TIMP-2 both restrains it and helps activate pro-MMP-2. An enzyme and its brakes rising together suggests the cell is not pushing in a single direction but switching on a remodelling programme as a whole.

The mare placenta is of the diffuse type, with the chorion attached to the uterine epithelium across its surface through microcotyledons. How that attachment is managed at the level of collagen and basement membrane shows up in the clinic mostly after foaling, when a retained placenta becomes a serious emergency. The paper does not study that, though it is the first question a veterinarian would ask about an MMP/TIMP balance. What it does is record how the tissue looks at term, and its value is as a reference line.

## Transcript up, protein unchanged

This is the part that stopped me. At the protein level, MMP-2, MMP-9, TIMP-1 and TIMP-2 are similar in both horns, and so are the MMP-2/TIMP-2 and MMP-9/TIMP-1 ratios. In the discussion the authors say that methylation may play a role in placental gene regulation. Reading that sentence, I wanted to underline the protein data more heavily.

A matrix proteinase acts through the ratio of active enzyme to inhibitor, and its mRNA is only the starting point. If the MMP-2/TIMP-2 balance is the same in both horns, the net effect on collagen turnover may be similar too. The transcript difference would then be a fluctuation with no biological consequence, or a signal whose protein echo arrives at another time. A placenta near term gives one moment; protein half-life and secretion rate can lag behind it or run ahead of it. Western blotting is also semi-quantitative, and a small protein difference in 17 animals could easily slip through.

The VEGF system did not move at all. VEGFA, VEGFR1 and VEGFR2 are the same in both horns. The title of the paper covers microvascularization, and I find this negative result as informative as the positive ones. If the pregnant horn had a greater need for vessels I would have expected the VEGF axis to be busier there. At term the vascular programme of the two horns may no longer differ, or the difference may sit somewhere other than transcript level, in vessel density or receptor activation. The data cannot tell the two apart.

A western blot also shows total protein rather than active enzyme. MMP-2 is made as a proenzyme and becomes active after cleavage. Gelatin zymography, or in situ zymography on tissue sections, would go a step beyond protein ratios and report how much collagen is actually being degraded in each horn. The abstract mentions no such measurement.

Immunohistochemistry adds some clarity. MMP-2, MMP-9, TIMP-1 and VEGF-A sit in trophoblast cells, whereas TIMP-2 is in the stroma of the chorioallantoic membrane. Two compartments of one tissue therefore play different roles: the trophoblast makes both enzyme and brake, and the stromal TIMP-2 comes from a different population of cells. Bulk-tissue qPCR squeezes these compartments into a single number. A transcript difference between PH and NPH could also arise from differences in cell number or cell composition, and the abstract does not let me rule that out.

A methylation percentage is hard to interpret without knowing how it is produced. I had never used pyrosequencing, so I first had to work out its mechanics. Bisulfite treatment converts unmethylated cytosines to uracil, which become thymine after PCR, while methylated cytosines stay as cytosine. Pyrosequencing reads the cytosine-to-thymine ratio at each CpG in a short amplicon, and the result is reported as a percentage methylation for that site. The number therefore describes the average state of a few specific CpGs. The rest of the promoter, the enhancers and the gene body fall outside the measurement.

There is a tissue problem as well. A placenta is a mixture of trophoblast, stromal cells, endothelium and immune cells, and a bisulfite measurement pools their methylation into one mean. MMP2 could be methylated one way in trophoblast and another way in stroma. If the pregnant horn carries a higher share of trophoblast, a lower average methylation could come from the shifted cell mixture alone, whatever the epigenetic state of the individual cells. The immunohistochemistry shows that several cell types are involved, since TIMP-2 sits in the stroma and the other four proteins in the trophoblast. Even if composition is identical in the two horns, I cannot close that question from the abstract.

The qPCR side hides something the abstract does not mention: which reference gene the transcripts were normalised to. In a tissue with such variable composition, the choice of reference gene bears directly on the result. If the reference gene itself shifts between horns, three targets could look "up" together as an artefact, and three genes rising in parallel could be carrying the trace of one normalisation bias. I do not write this as a flaw, because I cannot see the full text and the authors very probably dealt with it. It is one of the questions a reader should put to an abstract.

## A methylation number and a flipped sign

In the PH, hypomethylation of the MMP2, TIMP1 and TIMP2 promoters coincides with high transcript, and in the NPH hypermethylation coincides with low transcript. That fits the classical account, in which more promoter methylation means more silencing. Three genes pointing the same way supports that reading. At the level of horn averages the pattern is consistent; trouble starts when we descend to individual mares.

The story breaks when the authors turn to correlations. In the PH, MMP2 transcription correlates positively with methylation, so a mare with more methylation reads out more MMP2. In the NPH the correlation for MMP2, TIMP1 and TIMP2 is negative, which is the expected direction. The same gene behaves with opposite signs in the two horns.

That need not be an error or a contradiction. In his review of what DNA methylation does, Jones stresses that the relationship between methylation and transcription depends on genomic context: methylation at a transcription start site can say one thing and methylation inside a gene body another. A meta-analysis of human gene-body methylation reported that the relationship with expression is not monotonic, with the highest methylation in genes expressed at intermediate levels and low methylation in both the weakest and the strongest expressers. But Dos Santos and colleagues looked at promoter CpG islands. The gene-body story does not explain this positive correlation, and at best it offers a distant analogy.

A plainer explanation exists too. Pyrosequencing reads a short amplicon, and not every CpG in it matters equally for the gene. With 17 animals and a separate correlation for each horn, a few outliers can flip a sign. The authors write that an epigenetic mechanism "might be involved", and that wording is appropriate. To claim that methylation is causal I would want, at the very least, a cell culture in which promoter methylation is manipulated and transcription followed. Two tissue means falling side by side are a good first clue, not proof.

## A familiar gene pair from endometrosis

The choice of these genes comes from a well-trodden path in the mare literature, where the MMP/TIMP balance has long been studied in the endometrium and in endometrosis, the fibrotic degeneration of the uterine lining. Centeno and colleagues, working with 34 mare endometria, found collagen measured by picrosirius red staining at 11.72% in healthy tissue, 17.76% in moderate fibrosis and 24.15% in severe fibrosis; in the severe group MMP-2 transcript was higher and TIMP-2 lower. The same gene pair thus turns up as an axis that tips toward MMP-2 in fibrosis.

Amaral and colleagues showed that ovarian steroids can directly change MMP and TIMP expression in endometrial fibroblasts, and that progesterone reduced TGF-β1-induced collagen 1 accumulation in the mid-luteal phase. The review by Piotrowska-Tomala and colleagues summarises how DNA methylation, histone modifications and non-coding RNAs change in the endometrium and myometrium as endometrosis progresses.

Leal and colleagues examined the proteome of mare endometrium with endometritis and fibrosis. Inflammation-related proteins rose regardless of fibrosis grade, while histone H2A went up in mildly fibrotic tissue with endometritis and down in moderately fibrotic tissue with endometritis. At the protein level, then, the tissue can say something the transcript alone does not, and sometimes that something concerns chromatin.

This context adds another reading of the placenta paper. The pregnant horn is a very different environment from the empty horn in terms of hormones and mechanical load. If the MMP2/TIMP2 axis that marks fibrosis in the endometrium also moves physiologically in the placenta, then its activity alone cannot be proof of pathology, because the same gene pair works in healthy remodelling too. Whether the hypomethylation in the placenta reflects the hormonal milieu or a separate imprint left by the foal's presence is also not something these data can say.

## The question stays in the placenta

There are 17 mares, each with her own matched pair. That is enough to catch large effects. It may not be enough to detect a small difference in genes that came out as "no difference", such as MMP9 or the VEGF genes; no difference found is not the same as no difference. There is also a single time point, the placenta near term. Has the hypomethylation of the pregnant horn been there since early pregnancy, or was it laid down in the last weeks? Answering that takes placentas from different gestational stages, and that material is not easy to collect.

The full text of the paper is not open access and I could read only the PubMed abstract. So I do not know the correlation coefficients, the p-values, or how many CpGs the pyrosequencing covered. What I do know is this: transcript of the three genes is higher in the PH, protein ratios are identical in the two horns, and methylation is the source both of the tidy picture and of its contradiction. The weakest link is the one that separates correlation from causation. An experiment methylating the MMP2 promoter in equine trophoblast cells and following transcription would close it. Repeating the same measurements in placentas from mares with endometrosis would show whether the methylation difference travels with the disease. Until one of those is done, nobody knows whether the hypomethylation in the pregnant horn is a cause or a trace left by the foal.
