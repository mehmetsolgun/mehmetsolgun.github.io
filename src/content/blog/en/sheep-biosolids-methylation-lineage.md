Of 25,374 distinct methylation changes found in lamb liver, 93.4% turned up in the offspring of a single sire. That number from Hargreaves and colleagues, published in *PNAS Nexus* in October 2026, is the one that stayed with me, because the paper was built to answer a different question: if a pregnant ewe takes in a low-dose chemical cocktail, can anything be found on the DNA of her lambs and grand-lambs (Hargreaves et al., 2026)?

The exposure itself is almost mundane. Ewes (F0, 320 animals) grazed from six weeks before mating until lambing on either control pasture, fertilised with inorganic fertiliser, or pasture treated with biosolids, the processed by-product of sewage treatment that is spread on farmland around the world. Biosolids carry phthalates, per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), flame retardants, pharmaceuticals and organochlorine pesticides, each at low concentration and all at once. That is the opposite of the usual rodent experiment, where one chemical is given at a high dose, and it is why the model is attractive as a stand-in for human exposure. Groups using this model had already reported effects on the testis, puberty and ovarian reserve of the offspring. A study by Thangaraj and colleagues on lamb liver sits on the same line: control lambs had 647 sex-biased genes, yet in lambs from biosolid pastures 10 genes were differentially expressed in males and none in females (Thangaraj et al., 2025).

## Four rams, four pedigrees

To follow three generations, the team planned the matings in advance. F0 ewes were artificially inseminated with semen from four unrelated, unexposed rams (A, B, C and D), so control and biosolid animals came as half-sib families. Eight F1 rams, four from each treatment, then inseminated 149 F1 ewes from the other families, and F2 animals were mated naturally within their treatment groups to produce F3. Family matings were avoided. Pregnancy rate, litter size and sex ratio did not differ between treatments.

DNA methylation was read by reduced-representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS) in liver from F1, F2 and F3, in sperm from F1 and F2, and in F2 blood. A differentially methylated locus (DML) had to differ by at least 15% between treatments. At first glance the F1 liver results look encouraging: 25,374 unique DML in 16 offspring. But when the loci were sorted by sire, 93.4% belonged to one sire only, and when sorted by sex, 98.9% of 3,725 DML belonged to one sex only. Hierarchical clustering told the same story. Sire separated the animals first, sex came next, and treatment came last.

The authors say this plainly themselves: within a generation, 70 to 94% of DML were specific to one sire lineage and 88 to 96% to one sex. The effect of the sludge sits as a thin layer over genetic ancestry and sex. Its average size is small and similar in both sexes; in F1 liver, net methylation rose by 6.9% in exposed males and 7.4% in exposed females.

So the findings that reach F3 need to be read with care. F3 liver (16 males, 16 females) held 20,116 DML when partitioned by great-grandsire lineage, again 89.5% lineage-specific. The sign of the net effect had flipped: up by 2.0% in males, down by 7.1% in females. No locus was shared across all three generations in both sexes. Only in males did a single genic region, in a novel gene, appear in every generation.

## A different average in every generation

By F2 the picture shifts again. F2 liver (15 males, 16 females) yielded 50,700 DML when partitioned by grandsire, 85.6% of them specific to one lineage; F2 blood (22 males, 22 females) held 76,059 DML, 71.7% specific to one lineage. The sign of the net effect of the sludge changed with tissue and sex: in liver, a 0.4% decrease in males and a 4.2% increase in females; in blood, a 10.1% decrease in males and a 3.0% increase in females. The upward average seen in both sexes in F1 splits into opposite directions for males and females in F2 and F3. The same sludge, a generation later and in another tissue, does not fit a sentence that says "raises methylation" or "lowers methylation".

If you are looking for a mark that has travelled through the germline, the place to look is the link from F1 sperm to F2 tissues, and from F2 sperm to F3 liver. The numbers are small there too: 1,794 DML in F1 sperm (1,125 of them genic) overlap with DML in F2 liver, but only 76 DML are shared between F1 and F2 sperm, just under 2% of DML according to the authors and mostly intergenic. Twelve genes carry DML in both sperm sets. The authors write that with the data in hand they cannot tell true inheritance from coincidental coverage or from meQTL-driven methylation.

Lineage-by-lineage tracking lands in the same place. Among descendants of each of the four founder rams, the number of DML present in all three generations was 100 (in 16 genes) for lineage A, 107 (17 genes) for B, 90 (15 genes) for C and 91 (18 genes) for D. Not a single DML is common to all four lineages. Eleven genes carry DML in every lineage and every generation, but the exact positions differ from one generation to the next. The authors read that as the genome having sludge-sensitive regions rather than as a fixed mark being handed down; the functional groups among F3 liver genes with altered methylation point the same way, gathering around cell structure and adhesion, cell survival, development and differentiation in males, and around adhesion and migration, development, morphogenesis and signalling in females (35 enriched terms).

## Six miRNAs, one in sperm

The part that held my attention was not the liver, it was the sperm and the seminal plasma. F1 rams from the biosolid group had less methylated sperm, with 1,350 DML and a net fall of 7.2% when averaged across sires. Small-RNA sequencing of the same rams' sperm and seminal plasma aligned to 153 mature miRNA sequences from miRBase, and six passed the thresholds (log fold change of at least 2.0, FDR no higher than 0.01): miR-376e-5p, miR-376b-5p, miR-362, miR-544-5p, miR-539-5p and miR-376d. All six were differentially expressed in seminal plasma; only miR-376b-5p was also changed in sperm.

Two details stood out. Four of them, miR-544-5p, miR-376b-5p, miR-376e-5p and miR-376d, lie close together in the DLK1-GTL2/MEG3 imprinted domain on sheep chromosome 18, the region that controls the callipyge muscle-hypertrophy phenotype. The authors found no methylation difference at that locus in their somatic tissues and take this as consistent with muscle-specific regulation. And miR-539-5p is known in mice to act on a target that shapes DNA methylation. A miRNA that touches the methylation machinery, appearing in the same animals whose sperm is hypomethylated, makes an appealing story. The paper contains no experiment that connects the two, and the authors do not draw the connection.

In F2 rams no miRNA passed the criteria. Motility data point the same way: sperm from exposed F1 rams had a higher percentage of progressive motility (p = 0.044) and a faster straight-line velocity, 105.9 ± 4.88 µm/s against 86.7 ± 6.87 µm/s in controls (p = 0.042). Concentration did not change, and in F2 the difference vanished. The effects are measurable from father to son and fade from son to grandson. In the authors' own wording that is intergenerational, not transgenerational.

The idea that sperm RNA can carry information is not new. Chen and colleagues showed that in male mice on a high-fat diet, the expression and modification profile of sperm tRNA-derived small RNAs (tsRNAs) of 30 to 34 nucleotides shifted, that injecting these tsRNA fractions into normal zygotes produced metabolic disorder in F1 offspring, and that this was unrelated to DNA methylation at CpG-enriched regions (Chen et al., 2016). Comparing sperm RNA across species is a technical problem in itself. Schuster and colleagues reported that yields and subspecies of sperm-borne RNA vary drastically with the isolation method, which is why they built a single protocol and compared mouse, rat, rabbit and human sperm side by side (Schuster et al., 2016). I do not know of an equivalent standard for sheep, and an alignment to 153 miRNAs is not a full catalogue of ovine sperm small RNA.

## A threshold, a trap and two candidate genes

The question that Heard and Martienssen put to the field in *Cell* in 2014 still applies: the environment can certainly change gene expression, but transgenerational consequences are another matter, and how far they occur in humans remains unclear (Heard and Martienssen, 2014). Hargreaves and colleagues set the bar with three conditions: the mark must pass through the germline to the first unexposed generation (F3 here), the same epimutation must recur there, and gene expression must differ alongside it. They measure their results against these three and conclude that the evidence is not definitive.

That candour is warranted. RRBS preferentially captures CpG-dense regions such as islands and promoter-proximal sequence; hepatic DML fell 43.0% on islands, 11.0% on shores, 5.2% on shelves and 40.5% in open sea. Distal enhancers and repeats are largely out of view. The authors filtered CpGs that overlap known single-nucleotide polymorphisms and matched sire lineages across treatments, yet they acknowledge that this cannot remove allele-specific methylation or methylation quantitative trait loci (meQTL). A causal variant that was never assayed could shift methylation nearby and look like an effect of the sludge even when the CpG itself is unchanged.

Two loci are nonetheless interesting. Genes that escape germline demethylation, so-called escapees, have been defined in the pig. Of the published list of 101, the 93 named genes were crossed with F3 liver DML, and escapees were enriched for DML with an odds ratio above 5.1 against ordinary genes (p < 0.01). One of them, a tumour-suppressor gene, carries 10 DML packed into 169 bases of intron 4. The cluster was seen in at least one lineage in all three generations; in F3 males methylation was on average 9% higher and expression lower (p = 0.043), and in females neither changed. The second is a gene in the pseudo-autosomal region of the X chromosome that works in the dolichol pathway. Seven DML within just 39 bases of intron 3 recurred across three generations in lineages A, B and C. In F3 males methylation at those seven sites was 28% lower and expression higher (p < 0.01), with no change in females. But the pattern held in every generation of lineages B and C and failed in lineage A, which the authors read as a sign that part of the regulation is genetic.

For me that last detail is the most informative. A locus that behaves differently in lineage A looks more like a property of the pedigree than an effect of the sludge. A methylation difference that holds in three lineages cannot, with this design, be apportioned between pedigree and exposure, and the authors propose reciprocal matings across lineages plus dense genotyping and whole-genome sequencing as the next step.

The same animals show sex-specific effects in other organs too. In the ovaries of exposed offspring, Zhou and colleagues found higher apoptosis and autophagy and lower proliferation in juveniles (9.5 weeks old), but in adults (2.5 years) lower autophagy and higher ferroptosis (Zhou et al., 2025). Khan and colleagues reported larger left-ventricular dimensions, volumes and cardiac output in exposed adult males, while in females heart-rate variability pointed to weaker autonomic regulation (Khan et al., 2025). The same exposure seems to do something different in every organ and in each sex.

One more detail sits in the paper's own discussion: in the wider F1 cohort, plasma and liver triglycerides were higher in exposed males, and the functional groups of genes with altered hepatic methylation in males gathered under lipid and redox metabolism. Which locus drives which phenotype is still open. The authors say that linking metabolic and reproductive phenotypes in F2 and F3 animals to the methylation data is the subject of ongoing work. When it arrives, the first question I would ask is how many of the loci that behave like lineage A, even in a pedigree-controlled design, are still standing.
