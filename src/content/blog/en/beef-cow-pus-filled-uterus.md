Someone went through the reproductive tracts of 200 Angus cows at a local abattoir, one after another. Nine of the animals looked fine from the outside: good body condition, normal ovaries, growing follicles, a corpus luteum. Their uteri were full of pus. The paper by Adnan and colleagues, published in *Frontiers in Veterinary Science* in October 2026, starts from that observation and notes the cows' ages, three to five years (Adnan et al., 2026).

Five of these cows were compared with five cyclic cows whose uteri were clean. Tissue came from the intercaruncular endometrium, and the two groups were matched for corpus luteum stage so that progesterone would not confound the contrast. The RNA-seq result was 954 differentially expressed genes, 538 up and 416 down. That is a big number. Nine pus-filled uteri out of 200 examined, about 4.5%, is also a figure that comes from abattoir sampling. It says nothing about which herds the animals came from, how long before slaughter they had calved, or how common the condition is in the real herd. The sequencing itself looks clean: samples had an RNA integrity number above 7, and about 92% of reads mapped uniquely to the bovine genome (92.71% in controls, 92.11% in infected cows). Big numbers only mean something once you read the tables behind them.

## Antibodies at the top of the list

Nine of the twenty most upregulated genes are immunoglobulin-related. IGHG1 sits near 10.5 log2 units above control, and a run of "Ig-like domain-containing" and "immunoglobulin heavy variable" gene models crowd around it. A signal of that size suggests a tissue carrying a lot of plasma cells. The authors describe the infected endometrium as filled with plasma cells, neutrophils and lymphocytes, and their pathway software predicts more plasma cells, B cells and T cells. I read the picture as a lesion that has settled in rather than a fresh flare. The paper does not say this; I am inferring it, and we have no information on how long these cows had been sick.

One oddity deserves mention. Three Ig-like gene models also appear among the twenty most downregulated genes, one of them at −11.85 at the very bottom of the list. When members of the same family top both ends, I would not put much weight on any single fold change. Reads from different clones or different gene models are probably getting mixed together. That too is my interpretation.

The pathway results are more familiar. Upregulated genes cluster in GO terms for inflammatory response (29 genes) and immune response (26), and in KEGG for cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction (25) and chemokine signalling (18). Many Toll-like, NOD-like and RIG-I-like receptor genes went up, and so did NF-κB1 and NF-κB2. This matches the scheme Sheldon and colleagues described in their 2009 review: endometrial cells sense bacterial DNA, lipids and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) through Toll-like receptors, then secrete cytokines, chemokines and antimicrobial peptides, and the chemokines call in neutrophils and macrophages (Sheldon et al., 2009). The list reproduces that scheme and adds little to it. For someone who already knows the scheme, the surprises are elsewhere.

They are in the genes that went down.

## Why the defence genes are on the way down

Look at the top of the downregulated list and you meet genes better known from nerves and sensory organs: the cyclic-nucleotide-gated channel CNGA1, cerebellin 4, the hearing-related TMIE, the serotonin receptor HTR1A, the homeobox gene GSX1. I do not know what they do in an endometrium, and the paper does not explain. Log2 changes around −8 raise the possibility that these genes are barely counted even in healthy tissue. The raw counts are not in the text I read, so this is a suspicion and not a finding.

More striking is that the same top-twenty list contains a beta-defensin gene (−7.60), a collectin-like gene (−7.00) and BPIFA1 (−6.65). All three belong to the mucosal front line of innate defence. Among the upregulated genes, on the other hand, the authors name lingual antimicrobial peptide, tracheal antimicrobial peptide and several beta-defensin family members. So within one tissue some defence genes shoot up and others collapse.

I suspect composition is part of the answer. The whole endometrial sample was ground up. When immune cells flood the tissue their transcripts climb, but when epithelial cells become a smaller fraction, epithelium-specific transcripts get diluted. Some of the "downregulated" genes may reflect fewer epithelial cells per gram of tissue and not lower expression per cell. Separating the two would need single-cell data or at least cell-sorted samples, and the paper has neither.

Galectins also appear among the risers. The authors report galectin-1 up and galectin-3 significantly up; galectin-3 regulates how T cells adhere to endothelium and to dendritic cells or macrophages. It can be read as a genetic trace of cells being recruited into the tissue, and it does not conflict with the plasma-cell reading.

The downward GO and KEGG terms fit that reading. Among the downregulated genes the leading biological process is oxidation–reduction (11 genes), the molecular functions are heme binding (9), iron ion binding (8) and monooxygenase activity (7), and metabolic pathways top KEGG with 41 genes. This is the housekeeping machinery of uterine epithelium. The authors barely discuss it; their text is about the immune response from start to finish. I think the silent half is the more informative one. What the disease does to the metabolic work of the uterus gets less attention than the rise in inflammatory genes, even though it may bear more directly on fertility.

Zhang and colleagues told a partly similar story at the protein level in bovine endometritis: 159 differentially expressed proteins in endometrium, 137 in plasma, with extracellular hydrolysis as the leading process (Zhang et al., 2017). The two studies look at different layers and do not land in the same place, and extracellular proteolysis is not the same thing as metabolism. Still, both find that a diseased endometrium changes in structure and not only in its inflammatory genes.

## Five cows per side, 954 genes

The headline count is not a measure of statistical strength. The threshold is written as a two-fold change with a Benjamini–Hochberg q below 0.05, yet the results section says log2 fold change greater than 2, which would be four-fold. The two are not the same, and the paper does not settle which was used. There are five animals per group, collected at an abattoir, and the authors' own limitations paragraph says sample size was set by what external facilities could provide.

The validation step gives an uneven result. The most up- and downregulated candidates from RNA-seq were tested by qPCR. For normalisation, β-actin was picked as most stable among GAPDH, β-actin and TBP; three candidates and two groups, with no stability algorithm mentioned. Of the upregulated candidates, nine were measured and only two, C15H11orf34 (PLET1) and SLPI, reached significance. Of ten downregulated candidates, at least eight were significantly lower: GSX1, TDGF1, HTR1A, BPIFA1, PIPOX, PRKCG, TMIE and ST8SIA6. qPCR confirmed the downward arm convincingly. For the upward arm the score was two out of nine, and in the version I could read the gene names for the other seven were stripped from the figure legend. That does not make the RNA-seq wrong; high-variance genes in groups of five often fail a t test. But "immune genes went up" by RNA-seq and "what qPCR firmly confirmed" are not the same claim.

Above the gene lists sit the IPA networks, which the authors admit rest largely on human databases. IPA placed 822 molecules into 488 pathways. Of these, 117 were predicted active and 32 inhibited, while 321 showed no discernible pattern, so two thirds of the pathways give no direction. For GO and KEGG enrichment the threshold for calling a term is only two genes, and whether terms were corrected for multiple testing cannot be read from the version I had. A tool that compares a list of nearly a thousand genes against thousands of terms will always find something; that "inflammatory response" came out on top is what one would expect from the content of the list. The top canonical pathway was the Th2 pathway. A cow with a pus-filled uterus might well show Th2 skew, but a pathway tool ranking a canonical pathway highly does not show the pathway is running.

## Which disease is this

In 2006 Sheldon and colleagues proposed plain clinical definitions of postpartum uterine disease. Clinical endometritis is purulent discharge in the vagina 21 days or more after calving; subclinical endometritis is a neutrophil percentage in uterine cytology above a threshold; pyometra is purulent material in the lumen with a persistent corpus luteum and a closed cervix (Sheldon et al., 2006). The Angus cows had pus in the uterus and a corpus luteum on the ovary. That sounds like pyometra at first glance. But cervical status is not reported, nor time since calving or calving history, and no cultures were taken; the authors list the unconfirmed pathogen among their limitations. The paper calls the condition "infected uterus" and does not label it as endometritis, pyometra or anything else.

The corpus luteum detail also deserves thought. By matching on luteal stage the authors meant to remove the immunosuppressive effect of progesterone from both groups; their introduction says high progesterone suppresses immunity and leaves the uterus open to infection. But Sheldon and colleagues argue that luteal phases are often prolonged in cows with uterine disease, probably because infection switches endometrial epithelial secretion from prostaglandin F to E and disrupts luteolysis (Sheldon et al., 2009). If so, the corpus luteum in a pus-filled uterus may be no coincidence and may be a consequence of the infection. Matching by stage is a sound way to be sure both groups sit in the same progesterone environment, yet the diseased group may be in that stage because of the disease.

Etiology is the other missing piece. Sheldon and colleagues name Escherichia coli, Arcanobacterium pyogenes and bovine herpesvirus 4 as causes of endometrial damage. In the new dataset the RIG-I-like receptor genes, which sense viral nucleic acids, rose, with DDX58 significantly, and the authors read this as antiviral sensing. With no pathogen testing, the data cannot say whether a virus was present or whether these receptors respond to bacterial RNA and DNA too.

That naming gap has practical consequences. Salilew-Wondim and colleagues found 203 genes different in clinical endometritis and only 28 in subclinical endometritis in Holstein cows 42–60 days postpartum, 26 of them shared (Salilew-Wondim et al., 2016). Setting this against 954 is tempting, but the studies differ in platform (microarray versus RNA-seq), breed type (dairy versus beef) and clinical definition. If the gap says anything, it may be that the lesion here is far heavier and perhaps more chronic. In bovine endometrial epithelial cells stimulated with LPS in culture, only 60 genes changed (Zhou et al., 2023). The distance between a cultured cell and a slab of tissue is a rough measure of how much the immune cells and stroma contribute.

The number I found most interesting in the Salilew-Wondim study concerns miRNAs more than genes. In the same animals, subclinical endometritis changed 28 genes but 102 miRNAs, with many members of the let-7 family among them; clinical endometritis involved 35 miRNAs (Salilew-Wondim et al., 2016). If there is more movement at the miRNA level than at the mRNA level in the subclinical stage, then a design that looks only at mRNA, as in Adnan and colleagues, may be missing the other half of the pus-filled uterus. A small RNA library from the same tissue would have allowed the question of how much of the 954-gene list could be accounted for by miRNAs.

Near the end, the authors suggest the study animals might become infertile after the infection. This dataset cannot test that, because the cows were slaughtered. Nine healthy-looking cows walked in with normal ovaries. Which of them would have conceived again, and which would have stayed in the herd another year carrying that pus, nobody knows.
