Ten micromolar erastin did not measurably reduce the viability of cultured sheep granulosa cells. Twenty micromolar did. A paper by Ge and colleagues at Northwest A&F University, published in *Biomolecules*, rests on the gap between those two doses (Ge et al., 2026). Its subject is ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) and its relation to ferroptosis, and its model is primary granulosa cells from sheep ovaries collected at a slaughterhouse. The tissue comparisons use ovaries from Hu sheep.

The background is brief. In meat sheep, litter size is an economic trait, and it follows from how many follicles reach ovulation. Follicular atresia is the ordinary culling process of every ovary, and when it goes too far it thins out the pool of ovulatory follicles. Granulosa cells feed the oocyte, respond to gonadotropins and make estradiol (E2) and progesterone (P4), and their death is at the centre of atresia. For years that death was described as apoptosis. Then ferroptosis arrived. In 2012 Dixon and colleagues showed that erastin, a compound lethal to RAS-mutant cancer cells, triggers an iron-dependent form of cell death distinct from apoptosis: it blocks cystine uptake, leaves a hole in the antioxidant defences, and iron-driven lipid peroxidation finishes the cell (Dixon et al., 2012). The same paper introduced ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) as a compound that stops the process.

## Between ten and twenty micromolar

The authors first screened erastin at 0, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 and 200 µM for 24 hours. Up to 10 µM the cells looked much the same. At 20 µM there were morphological abnormalities and lower cell density, reactive oxygen species (ROS) rose, and the CCK-8 viability assay fell significantly. From 50 to 200 µM the damage was so extensive that those doses were dropped. At both of the remaining doses, malondialdehyde (MDA) built up, glutathione (GSH) fell and E2 and P4 output dropped. The authors used 10 µM as mild ferroptotic stress and 20 µM as severe.

That distinction needs to be taken seriously, because the results do not come out the same at both doses. At 10 µM, FTH1 overexpression changed the mRNA levels of ferroptosis genes but had limited effects on MDA and GSH. At 20 µM the mRNA effects weakened and the MDA and GSH effects became clear. The authors explain this by saying that mRNA reflects regulation at a chosen moment, while MDA and GSH reflect accumulated damage. That is plausible. The same data could equally be read as an effect that behaves inconsistently with dose, and with a single time point the two readings cannot be pulled apart.

FTH1 was chosen because of an earlier single-cell transcriptome study from the same group: in ovaries of Hu sheep with different litter sizes, ferroptosis-related pathways differed, and FTH1 stood out as a candidate gene tied to granulosa function. The hypothesis follows from that. FTH1 limits the iron and oxidative damage caused by erastin, and so preserves viability and hormone output. On the tissue side, FTH1 was higher in ovaries from multiple-lamb Hu sheep than from single-lamb ones. In granulosa cells it was higher in large follicles (over 4.5 mm) than in small ones (under 3.0 mm), and higher in healthy than in atretic follicles. The numbers are small. The ovary comparison used three ovaries per group. The granulosa samples came from 100 ovaries of 50 ewes at a slaughterhouse, with 8 to 12 follicles pooled per sample, and the parity of the ewes was unknown. Atresia was classified by appearance, meaning the colour of the follicle, its vessels and the turbidity of its fluid, with apoptosis genes checked as support. FTH1 being higher in healthy follicles is reasonable, but it cannot say whether low FTH1 causes atresia or follows from it, and the authors say as much.

## Damage first, plasmid second

The order of the functional experiments is, to my mind, the most important detail in the paper, and it is easy to miss in the main text. According to the methods, cells were first exposed to erastin for 24 hours. Then the erastin-containing medium was removed, and the FTH1 overexpression plasmid or the siRNA was transfected; cells were collected 24 hours after transfection. The same sequence is described for the RNA-seq experiment. As I read it, this is not a prevention experiment. The damage was already done, erastin was no longer in the dish, and FTH1 was raised afterwards. What was measured was recovery. The paper says FTH1 "protects". I would say it accompanied recovery, because having the gene in place before the insult was not tested.

The results point in one consistent direction. Overexpression raised GPX4 protein at both doses (p < 0.01) and lowered ROS. At 20 µM it lowered MDA, raised GSH and reduced Fe²⁺. Knockdown did the opposite: GPX4 protein fell, MDA rose, GSH dropped. In cells that never saw erastin, overexpression still raised GSH and lowered MDA, total iron and Fe²⁺. That last finding deserves attention because it suggests the effect is not specific to erastin and looks like general iron buffering. FTH1 oxidises Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ and stores it inside ferritin, shrinking the free iron pool, and the data fit that textbook role.

Two details sit awkwardly. First, at 10 µM erastin, silencing FTH1 raised Fe²⁺ while lowering total iron. The authors read this as a change in the chemical form of iron plus possible compensation, but they did not measure iron uptake or export. Second, the transferrin receptor gene went up with overexpression even though intracellular Fe²⁺ went down. Their suggested explanation is that a shrinking iron pool stabilises the mRNA through the IRP–IRE system, and that too was not tested at the protein or IRP-binding level. More basically, there is no iron-chelator rescue. If ferroptosis is defined by iron dependence, a chelator is what would show the damage came from iron, and the authors list the omission among their own limitations.

On the statistics the authors took real care. Each biological replicate is a pooled granulosa culture from one of three independent ovary collections done on separate days, and microscope fields from one culture were averaged rather than counted as separate samples. Solvent was handled too: DMSO was matched in each comparison (between 0.1% and 0.3% in the erastin groups), with an empty pcDNA3.1 vector for the plasmid and a non-targeting control for the siRNA. Comparisons were made only between groups at the same erastin dose, with unpaired t tests and no comparison across doses. The Cq values of the RT-qPCR reference gene were tested separately in each of three experimental series with two-way ANOVA, and no treatment-related change was found; plenty of papers skip that step. On the other side, n is three everywhere, and dozens of separate t tests were read at p < 0.05 with no multiplicity correction. With three samples the distance between a difference that reaches p < 0.05 and one that does not is short, and many findings the text calls "trends" sit just below or above that line.

## Fer-1, hormones and apoptosis

A granulosa cell that does its job has to make steroids as well as stay alive. FTH1 overexpression raised STAR and CYP11A1 in the erastin-treated cells, two steps on the route from cholesterol to pregnenolone, and E2 and P4 concentrations were higher than in erastin-treated controls. Knockdown did the opposite. Without erastin, overexpression left basal E2 and P4 unchanged. So the gene does not stimulate hormone output; it reduces what the damage takes away from it. The authors explain this by protection of the mitochondrial steps of steroidogenesis. They also note that granulosa cells depend on androgen substrate from theca cells to make oestrogen, and that this supply is absent in an isolated granulosa model, so the E2 result should not be read as regulation of the whole oestrogen pathway.

Fer-1 was a separate experiment. Given together with erastin, 10 µM Fer-1 restored viability significantly at 20 µM erastin and raised the steroidogenic markers and E2 and P4. At 20 µM erastin it also lowered the apoptotic cell fraction and CASP3 expression; at 10 µM it moved in that direction without reaching significance. The authors state clearly that they have found an association between ferroptosis and apoptosis and have not shown that ferroptosis comes first.

None of this stands alone. In a study in *Poultry Science*, granulosa cells from atretic goose follicles had lower FTH1 and GPX4 mRNA and higher NCOA4. HMOX1 delivered in follicular-fluid exosomes reduced FTH1 and increased ferroptosis, and Fer-1 reversed the effect (Zhang et al., 2024). NCOA4 is the cargo receptor for the selective autophagy of ferritin, known as ferritinophagy. Hou and colleagues showed that overexpressing NCOA4 increased ferritin degradation and ferroptosis, while silencing it did the reverse (Hou et al., 2016). FTH1 therefore sits in the middle of an axis that controls ferroptosis from the side of storage and from the side of breakdown, and the sheep paper followed NCOA4 only as an mRNA marker, without measuring ferritinophagy itself. A related system gives a similar picture: in mice with cyclophosphamide-induced premature ovarian insufficiency, a herbal formula reduced both ferroptosis and apoptosis in granulosa cells (Huang et al., 2025). A review by Wang and colleagues stresses that several forms of programmed cell death run together in atresia (Wang et al., 2023).

Erastin itself sets a limit. It is a pharmacological blocker of the cystine transporter, and it hits the cell first by draining GSH. A granulosa cell in a real follicle may reach ferroptosis by other routes: in the goose study the trigger was exosomes from follicular fluid. That FTH1 helps cells recover after an erastin injury does not say what is missing in natural atresia. All measurements were also taken after a single exposure period. The authors' own limitations section admits that caspase activity, steroidogenic enzyme activity and CASP3 cleavage were not measured, so the biochemical changes at 10 µM and the morphological damage at 20 µM cannot be assigned to a time sequence or to a non-linear threshold.

## 0.98 in six samples

The RNA-seq compared 10 µM erastin with 10 µM erastin plus FTH1 overexpression, three samples per group. It annotated 18,988 genes. Of these, 137 mRNAs went up and 160 down, and 51 lncRNAs went up and 41 down, with a threshold of |log2 fold change| above 1.2. Upregulated genes were enriched in cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum and JAK-STAT signalling; downregulated genes were enriched in oxidative phosphorylation. The authors call the analysis exploratory, and that candour is right: there is no untreated control, so FTH1's own effect cannot be separated from its effect under stress.

What caught my attention was the lncRNA–mRNA correlation. Pearson correlations were computed across six samples. With |r| ≥ 0.60 and a nominal p < 0.05, 23 lncRNAs were reported as correlated with FTH1, 16 positively and seven negatively; the strongest had r = 0.980 (p = 0.001). But three of the six samples carry the plasmid, and FTH1 is the most markedly raised gene in those samples. In a design like that, any transcript that separates by group will correlate strongly with FTH1. That is the arithmetic of the design before it is evidence of regulation, and the paper itself says these results do not demonstrate direct regulatory relationships. It is right to say so, and the follow-on speculation about a miR-29 binding site stays inside the same limit.

From a veterinarian's seat, there are three steps between this culture system and what we meet in the clinic, namely low ovulation numbers and small litters: cell, follicle and animal. The paper stays inside the first, and the authors accept that by declining to claim the findings apply to reproductive management. The boundary is drawn correctly.

My order for the next experiments would be a chelator rescue first, then a design with plasmid and erastin in reverse order, then naturally atretic follicles. That FTH1 turned out higher in the ovaries of multiple-lamb Hu ewes is waiting on one of those three. For now what exists is three ovaries against three ovaries, and a gap between two doses.
