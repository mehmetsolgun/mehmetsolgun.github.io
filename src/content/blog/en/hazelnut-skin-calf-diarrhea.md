By day six, four calves had died in each group. In the control arm 26 of 30 were still alive; in the arm that received hazelnut skin extract, 26 of 30 as well. That tie is the number that stayed with me from the trial Boccardo and colleagues published in *Veterinary Quarterly* in October 2026, because the team had set out hoping for the opposite (Boccardo et al., 2026). Hazelnut skin is a by-product of the chocolate and spread industry. It is rich in phenolic compounds, it is cheap, and the idea of feeding an industrial waste stream back into animal production has an obvious appeal.

The hypothesis was reasonable. Diarrhoea is the leading cause of illness and death in pre-weaned dairy calves, and most cases end up on antimicrobials (Carter et al., 2021). As pressure on antibiotic use grows, plant-derived products look like a way out, yet almost nobody had tested them as an add-on treatment in calves sick enough to be admitted to a hospital. The Milan group went into that gap.

## Four and four by day six

The design is plain. Calves up to 28 days old, with a faecal score of at least 2, were admitted to the ruminant clinic of the University of Milan's veterinary school, and only those who needed intravenous fluids were enrolled. Mild cases were left out on purpose. Of 84 diarrhoeic calves presenting during the study, 24 failed the criteria; the remaining 60 female Holstein calves were randomised to two groups of 30. The extract group received 10 g of extract mixed into the morning milk for five days.

These were sick animals. Median blood pH at admission was 7.15 in controls and 7.14 in the extract group, and median base excess sat between −16.0 and −18.2 mmol/L. Nobody here was "a calf with a loose stool"; these were calves with marked metabolic acidosis, some unable to stand. Everybody got the same protocol: intravenous fluids, flunixin meglumine, vitamin E with selenium, thiamine, and long-acting amoxicillin at 150 mg/kg on admission and again 48 hours later.

Nothing separated the groups. Six-day weight gain was 2.04 ± 2.27 kg versus 2.08 ± 2.93 kg. Median hospital stay was 8 days in controls (interquartile range 7–11) and 9 in the extract group (8–12). Discharged alive: 23 versus 24; in total seven control calves and six extract calves died. Milk intake, hydration, suckle reflex, sepsis score and faecal score showed no group effect either. Time, by contrast, had an enormous effect on every outcome. At admission the odds of an impaired suckle reflex were 36 times higher than on day six (OR 36.00; 95% CI 11.09–116.89). The calves got better. They got better at the same speed in both groups.

This is where the study is at its most honest. The authors attribute the recovery to standard therapy and give the extract no share of it. To read that as "polyphenols do not work" would be a stretch, though; the trial was not built to answer that question, and I will come back to why.

## The red stool problem

The most practical thing I took from the paper is that the design could not be blinded from the start. The authors had planned a blinded assessment. The extract, however, stains faeces reddish, and the clinicians scoring the calves could see which animal belonged to which group at a glance. Because the result is null, this bias does no damage to the conclusion. Had the result been positive, it would have been the first objection. In a study that leans on faecal score, an intervention that changes the colour of the thing being scored is an intervention that changes the measurement.

Set it beside the two earlier trials. Bonelli and colleagues, working with 24 calves, found that 10 g of chestnut tannin extract shortened the diarrhoeic episode, 10.1 ± 3.2 days versus 6.6 ± 3.8 days (p = 0.02), a difference of almost four days (Bonelli et al., 2018). In that trial the control group received only an electrolyte preparation (Effydral) in 2 litres of warm water, and the tannin group the same solution with 10 g of extract added; the question was an add-on to fluid therapy, without hospitalisation, antibiotics or intravenous fluids as confounders. The Milan authors point out that the Bonelli cohort was restricted to non-severe cases, whereas theirs was made up of calves needing intravenous fluids. Two trials, the same 10 g per day, two levels of disease severity, two answers. Matching the grams is not enough; what is in each gram has to be known as well.

A hospitalised calf is also a different animal from a field case. In a calf on intravenous fluids with its acidosis being corrected, most of the improvement in faecal score is the work of the fluids and the antibiotic. Smith's review of antimicrobial decisions for enteric disease in cattle makes the point that whether antibiotics are truly needed has to be argued case by case (Smith, 2015). Boccardo's team chose to give amoxicillin to everyone, because hospitalised calves face a higher risk of bacteraemia. That choice has an internal logic: had only one group received antibiotics, any difference in faecal score could have come from the drug.

The sepsis score belongs in the same frame. It is built on the clinical score that Fecteau and colleagues developed in 1997 from 90 ill calves; in that work 31% of calves were bacteraemic on blood culture and the model predicted bacteraemia with a sensitivity of 75% and a specificity of 71% (Fecteau et al., 1997). A tool that is right about three times in four is too coarse to pick up a small difference between two groups of 30.

## Seventeen models and one borderline p value

The methods section says a separate GEE model was built for each outcome and lists them: vitality, suckle reflex, attitude, faecal score, hydration, episcleral vessel congestion, ocular and ear findings, nasal discharge, cough, umbilical condition, sepsis score, sepsis percentage, plus body weight, milk intake, rectal temperature, OXY and d-ROMs. That makes seventeen models. No correction for multiple comparisons is mentioned. Since nothing came out significant, this does no harm; still, it is worth remembering that the single borderline value, p = 0.050, is one of seventeen tries. In a positive study the same table would be read very differently.

Baseline balance offers a similar comfort, but not a complete one. Despite randomisation, some differences remained in groups of 30. At admission median rectal temperature was 38.8 °C in controls and 39.5 °C in the extract group; lactate was 1.78 versus 1.14 mmol/L (p = 0.073); enterotoxigenic or Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* was detected in three of 28 control calves and in none of the extract group (p = 0.080). None of these is significant. The direction is not even consistent: the extract group ran slightly hotter but had slightly lower lactate. In small groups, non-significant differences can add up and push a result one way, and the most honest thing to do in this kind of trial is to publish the table as it is. The authors did.

## One extract, two doses

The extract was produced by microwave-assisted subcritical water extraction, with a yield of nearly 25%. The part of the paper I found strangest is the description of the extract itself. In the methods, total phenolic content is given as 778.4 ± 16.0 mg gallic acid equivalents per gram of dry extract, and the daily polyphenol dose per calf is calculated as 7,784 mg. In the discussion, the same extract is described as having a polyphenol concentration of 850 mg/g, and the dose is 8,500 mg per calf, roughly 20 mg/kg. The two figures differ by about 9%. I do not know which one is right, so I report both as they stand.

More important is what sits in the same methods section: the sum of the individually measured compounds is much smaller. Phenolic acids are 1.36 mg/g, flavan-3-ols 14.84 mg/g, flavonols 0.51 mg/g. Together that is about 16.7 mg per gram, roughly 2% of the Folin-type total. This is not necessarily an error. Total phenolic assays also count reducing substances that are not polyphenols, and the compounds identified one by one may cover only part of the extract. Still, saying that a calf received "7,784 mg of polyphenols" suggests that this much defined, biologically meaningful compound went into the animal, and the data in the paper do not support that impression.

The authors' own explanation for the lack of effect has two parts: five days is short, and the disease is severe. Studies in healthy calves that reported benefits gave polyphenols for 15 to 70 days. A calf with acute diarrhoea does not offer that window. It is a bind. For a therapeutic effect the supplement must act within days; for a protective effect it must be given for weeks, and the two do not meet.

## What the antioxidant tests could see

The oxidative stress readout consists of two assays: the OXY test, which measures the capacity of serum to neutralise hypochlorous acid, and d-ROMs, which measures derivatives of reactive oxygen metabolites. On day six, OXY was 385.1 ± 86.3 in controls and 371.5 ± 100.2 µmol HClO/mL in the extract group. At admission the figures were 345.7 ± 77.3 and 369.0 ± 89.1. So the control group rose, and the extract group stayed where it was. In the generalised estimating equation the group effect came out at p = 0.050, which the paper calls borderline, and the effect size is reported as "IRR −28.69 (95% CI −57.37 to −0.009)". A ratio of rates cannot be negative; this is most likely a regression coefficient. If the sign is right, the gap is against the extract group, the opposite of the hypothesis, but I would not dare to interpret that number on its own.

The d-ROMs data are even more scattered. Medians sit between 21 and 27, but the interquartile range in the control group on day six runs from 13.20 to 135.90. A measurement whose upper quartile is ten times its lower one cannot reveal a small shift in a group of 30 animals.

One thing remains unresolved for me: every calf received parenteral vitamin E and selenium. Both are core pieces of antioxidant defence. The authors do not discuss this; it is my reading. But if all calves got the same antioxidant-rich treatment and the endpoint is serum antioxidant capacity, a small contribution from the extract would be hard to see on that background.

The sample size raises a similar worry. The power calculation used an effect size of 0.3, α = 0.05 and 80% power, and returned 60 animals. For a two-sample t test with those parameters, the standard formula gives about 175 animals per group, around 350 in total. I note this as my own calculation; the authors' software may have assumed a different test. With groups of 30, only a very large difference could have been detected, and nobody should have expected the extract to produce one.

Zhai and colleagues' work with nine probiotic strains isolated from healthy calves is a reminder that plant extracts are not the only alternative: treated calves shed healthy faeces and showed a restored gut microbiota (Zhai et al., 2024). It is also a small experiment, but it aims at the microbiota directly instead of assuming that polyphenols reach it. Carter and colleagues' review proposes filling the same gap with colostrum: rich in immunoglobulins, natural antimicrobial factors and growth factors, it is presented as an attractive alternative to antimicrobials, although its therapeutic and economic effectiveness still has to be investigated.

Back to hazelnut skin, then. A five-day course and a severely ill cohort are only part of the problem. Would a trial in mild field cases, with proper blinding and an extract whose composition is fully characterised, change the answer? With today's data, the only thing I can say is that the one visible effect of the extract in a sick calf was the colour of its stool.
