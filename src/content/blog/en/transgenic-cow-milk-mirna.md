Sixty micrograms. That is how much transgenic cow milk vesicle Husseini and colleagues gave their Caco-2 gut cells, and the reporter gene inside those cells was not silenced. A few pages later in the same paper, the authors calculate what it would take to silence it: at least 100 micrograms of the richest vesicle fraction, and between 500 and 4,000 micrograms of the others. The study, published in *FASEB Journal* in September 2026 (Husseini et al., 2026), is therefore less a negative result than a demonstration of why this particular negative result does not yet say much.

The problem the study set out to solve is an old one. Bovine miR-148a or let-7b are almost identical in sequence to their mouse and human counterparts. If you see more of one in a mouse intestine after the animal drank milk, you cannot tell whether it came from the milk or from the mouse. The team tried to get around that with a cow whose milk carries four foreign sequences.

## Four foreign sequences, one cow

The model goes back to 2012, when Jabed and colleagues at AgResearch in New Zealand expressed miRNAs in cattle to silence β-lactoglobulin (BLG), a major milk allergen. Hormonally induced milk from the transgenic calf had no BLG, and all casein proteins went up (Jabed et al., 2012). That work was about changing milk composition. Husseini's group took two females of the F1 generation of that line and used the engineered miRNAs the other way round. AmiR-3 and AmiR-4, plus their star strands AmiR-3* and AmiR-4*, became tracers, because nothing in the animals used for later experiments carries sequences like them.

Most miRNAs in milk come from the mammary epithelium, so the foreign ones turned up in the milk as expected. The authors measured absolute copy numbers by RT-qPCR with a UniSp2 spike-in for normalisation. AmiR-3 was the most abundant of the four, and all four sat well above miR-148a and let-7b, which are usually counted among the ten most abundant milk miRNAs. Small RNA sequencing then showed something less convenient: the transgene had not only added its own products but had shifted the classical milk repertoire. Reads for miR-99a-5p fell tenfold relative to wild-type milk, and let-7f rose fourfold. The model is a source of foreign miRNA, and also a cow whose miRNA balance has moved.

The statistics fit that scale: transgenic and wild-type samples were compared with unpaired t tests, with p values corrected for multiple comparisons by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, and the digestion experiment is reported only as means and standard deviations. The sample is small, and the authors say so. Transgenic milk came from a single cow (number 1508) at three different time points, wild-type milk from three separate cows, one lot each. "n = 3" on the transgenic side therefore means one animal sampled three times. I read every distribution result below as a trend and not as a precise percentage.

## Which pellet holds the miRNA

The standard way to separate milk extracellular vesicles (EVs) is differential ultracentrifugation. Spinning faster pulls down different populations: 12,000, 35,000, 70,000 and 100,000 × g give what the authors call the 12K, 35K, 70K and 100K pellets. For years the field mostly studied the 100K pellet, the smallest fraction, usually called exosomes. A 2017 paper from the same laboratory, published in the *Journal of Extracellular Vesicles*, questioned that habit. In commercial cow milk the bulk of the miRNA sedimented at 12,000 and 35,000 × g, with a distribution that looked more like milk fat globule membrane proteins such as xanthine dehydrogenase (XDH) than like exosome markers. These vesicles were around 100 nm in diameter and protected their RNA from RNase A (Benmoussa et al., 2017).

The new paper repeats the exercise for raw, unpasteurised milk and finds the foreign miRNAs following the same pattern. All four AmiRs were most abundant in the 12K pellet, dropped step by step as the centrifugal force rose, and were lowest in the supernatant after the final 100,000 × g spin. miR-148a and let-7b behaved the same way. For someone who works on milk miRNA as a biomarker for mastitis or pregnancy, this is not a small detail. Many biomarker studies still stop at the 100K pellet or at a commercial exosome kit. If most of the miRNA is in 12K and 35K, such protocols sample a minority of it. I do not read this as a refutation of any method. It is a reminder that each study should measure its own pellet distribution at least once.

The pellets differ in more than quantity. The same team's 2019 small RNA sequencing in the *Journal of Dairy Science* found that five sequences accounted for more than half of all miRNAs in commercial cow milk, that the 100K pellet was enriched in reference sequences, and that the 12K and 35K pellets were enriched in their isomiR variants (Benmoussa et al., 2019a). Skipping those pellets therefore discards diversity as well as copies. There is a hint on the biological side too. In a mouse model of experimental colitis, the same group reported that the 35K pellet eased symptoms more clearly than the 100K pellet, and restored gut barrier function and mucin secretion (Benmoussa et al., 2019b). That is indirect evidence that the larger vesicles do something in the gut; which cargo is responsible remains open.

One note on the vesicles themselves. Under the electron microscope the vesicles had the typical cup shape and were quite heterogeneous in size, both between pellets and within a single pellet. On western blots ALIX, TSG101, CD63, Hsp70 and XDH were found in both wild-type and transgenic pellets, at levels that varied from pellet to pellet. Hydrodynamic size by dynamic light scattering did not differ between matching transgenic and wild-type pellets. Particle counts, however, were higher in every transgenic pellet, and protein yield was higher too, significantly so for 12K and 70K. So the transgenic milk differs in vesicle yield as well as in miRNA balance. The authors mention in the discussion that transgenesis might have altered vesicle surface proteins or uptake routes. That is a guess; it was not tested.

## What comes out the other end

To act in a consumer's cells, a miRNA first has to survive the stomach and the small intestine. The team used the TNO TIM-1 system, a computer-controlled model with separate compartments for the stomach, duodenum, jejunum and ileum, and simulated two hours of adult digestion. The same laboratory had shown in 2016 that bta-miR-223 and bta-miR-125b in commercial cow milk largely survive this treatment, with the stomach causing the biggest loss (Benmoussa et al., 2016). According to the new paper's discussion, 12 percent of the first and 26 percent of the second made it through two hours.

The foreign sequences did not survive equally. The amount caught at the final exit, the effluent, was about 10 percent of the starting input for AmiR-4*, about 3.5 percent for AmiR-4 and AmiR-3*, and for AmiR-3 more than 97.3 percent was lost. Among classical milk miRNAs, miR-146b kept 35 percent and miR-200a kept 1 percent. Same milk, same digestion, and a 35-fold spread among the classical ones alone. The authors point to secondary structure, sequence composition, GC content or bound proteins, but test none of them. I take the spread as a hint that "vesicle membrane protects miRNA" is an incomplete sentence. If the membrane did all the work, why would survival depend on the sequence? Perhaps miRNAs that end up in the same pellet are not all sitting in the same compartment.

The digestion data come from two runs (n = 2), which the authors list among their limitations. The losses were large, but each AmiR still reached the effluent in measurable copy numbers. A miRNA being present in the gut lumen is one thing; getting into a cell is another. The authors argue that surviving miRNAs could accumulate with repeated milk intake, and that even amounts too low to spread through the body might act locally on intestinal cells. They also concede the other side of the arithmetic: with roughly 90 percent lost in digestion, reaching an effective dose at the gut wall might take a supraphysiological amount of milk in a single sitting.

## The 60-microgram arithmetic

Two cell experiments follow. In the first, Caco-2 cells carrying a luciferase reporter (psiCHECK-2) with the AmiR-4 binding site were treated with 60 µg of transgenic EVs. The Renilla/firefly ratio dipped slightly in cells with the binding site, but not significantly against wild-type EVs or against cells given no EVs. A plasmid with a mutated binding site showed no change either. The authors read this as little or no AmiR transfer.

The second experiment is there to interpret the first. HEK293T cells received a plasmid producing β-lactoglobulin together with synthetic AmiR mimics, to find the smallest dose that silences. AmiR-3 lowered BLG protein at 100 and 50 pM, and lowered mRNA only at 100 pM. AmiR-4 worked at the protein level only at 100 pM. The synergy attributed to the pair by Jabed et al. did not appear: AmiR-3 alone silenced more than the two together.

Converting that threshold into vesicles gives the key figure. To reach the effective copy number you would need 100 µg of 12K EVs, 500 µg of 35K, 2,500 µg of 70K or 4,000 µg of 100K. The Caco-2 experiment used 60 µg. That is below what even the richest pellet requires and roughly 67 times below the poorest. After reading that line I could not see how the reporter result could be read as "no transfer". The dose was insufficient by the authors' own calculation. The calculation also rests on mimic transfection, and RNA delivered by a lipid reagent enters cells with a different efficiency from RNA delivered in a vesicle, which the authors say themselves. They cite work suggesting that only about 24 percent of internalised EVs release cargo into the cytoplasm, and that this happens in only about 5 percent of recipient cells. I did not check those figures and leave them with the paper's references, but if they hold, the real threshold sits higher still.

The cell line is another worry. Milk EVs have been reported to enter cells through caveolin-mediated endocytosis, and Caco-2 cells do not express caveolin-1. The authors list this as an important limitation. Part of the reporter experiment is thus a test of the cell model itself.

The result sits inside an old argument. Zhang and colleagues in Nanjing proposed in 2012 that rice MIR168a suppresses LDLRAP1 in mice (Zhang et al., 2012). Dickinson and colleagues tried to reproduce the idea with feeding experiments and found no detectable oral bioavailability of plant miRNAs (Dickinson et al., 2013). In Zurich, Title and colleagues asked the same question for milk by genetic means: miR-375 and miR-200c/141 knockout pups were fostered on wild-type mothers, and the intestinal epithelium, blood, liver and spleen showed no sign of uptake. The authors gave the threshold for gene regulation as 1,000 to 10,000 copies per cell and reported rapid degradation of milk miRNAs in intestinal fluid (Title et al., 2015). The 2019 study with Benmoussa's name on it reported the opposite in HeLa cells: milk EVs delivered miR-223 and lowered a miR-223-controlled reporter (Benmoussa et al., 2019a). So the same author has now reported a positive and a negative result. I do not see a contradiction. Different cell type, different EV subset, different dose, and the answer seems to move with all three.

What I would like to see next is the transgenic cow doing her real job. Because the foreign sequences cannot be confused with a mouse's or a calf's own miRNAs, giving this milk to animals and looking for AmiRs in the gut wall and the blood may finally be a clean experiment. If they show up, the claim that milk carries working miRNAs gets a firm footing. If they do not, reading milk miRNA as a signature of the udder's own state becomes much more comfortable, and for anyone hunting for a mastitis marker that is useful to know.
