In the blood of golden hamsters infected with Leptospira, the rise in TNF-α came out roughly ten times larger when the data were normalised to beta-actin (ACTB) than when they were normalised to cyclophilin A (PPIA). Same target, same animals, same RNA. The only thing that changed was the gene written under the fraction line. Nogueira and colleagues at the Federal University of Pelotas put this comparison at the end of what looks like a routine reference-gene screen, published in *Molecular Biology Reports* (Nogueira et al., 2026).

The golden hamster is the standard small-animal model of leptospirosis, and it is a poor one for protein work: hamster-specific antibodies and immunoassay reagents are scarce, so immune responses are mostly followed at the mRNA level by RT-qPCR. Blood is an attractive matrix for that. It can be sampled repeatedly and it pools the systemic picture in one tube. The same group had already chosen reference genes for hamster kidney, liver and lung. Blood, they argue, is a different problem, because leukocyte proportions shift during infection and cytoskeletal genes such as actin are reorganised when cells are activated. The argument is reasonable, but the paper gives no leukocyte counts or differential, so it stays a plausible hypothesis rather than a measured finding.

## Six groups, four animals each

The animals were 4–6-week-old male and female hamsters in six groups: infected with Leptospira interrogans serovar Copenhageni; immunised with ovalbumin on aluminium hydroxide (Al(OH)3); immunised with attenuated Salmonella Typhimurium; given an inactivated leptospiral bacterin; given BCG Pasteur; or injected with PBS as controls. That is four animals per group and twenty-four in total. Vaccinated hamsters were bled 21 days after the booster. Infected ones were bled when they reached predefined clinical endpoints such as haematuria, nasal bleeding, anorexia or more than 10% weight loss. Blood was taken by cardiac puncture straight into RNAlater.

RNA was isolated from whole blood with a protocol that combines TRIzol and RNeasy columns, and A260/280 ratios fell between 1.92 and 2.08. From each sample 1,000 ng of RNA was reverse-transcribed with random primers, and each reaction received 100 ng of cDNA, run with SYBR Green in duplicate. Amplicon specificity was shown by gel electrophoresis and melt curves. The main text reports spectrophotometric concentration and purity only; I did not find an RNA integrity value (RIN or similar), though it may sit in the supplement, which I did not read. In whole blood that matters, because in partly degraded RNA two genes with amplicons of different length drift to different Ct values, and any reference-gene ranking inherits that noise.

Eleven candidate genes were tested. Some had come out of earlier hamster RNA-seq work, some were non-conventional genes reported as stable in other models, and three were the classical workhorses most labs reach for first. Stability was scored with four algorithms (geNorm, NormFinder, BestKeeper and the comparative ΔCt method), and the four rankings were then combined by the RefFinder logic, a geometric mean of ranks, using an R package called RefSeeker.

Across all samples pooled, PPIA was the most stable gene, followed by GAPDH and PPIB. ACTB was the least stable of the eleven. The default gene in many laboratories finished eleventh out of eleven.

## Four algorithms, four rankings

What struck me in the tables was how little the algorithms agreed. In the bacterin group GAPDH is first by BestKeeper (mean absolute deviation 2.579) but ninth by the ΔCt method and ninth by NormFinder. In the PBS controls RPS16 is first by BestKeeper and eighth by NormFinder. Same data, same twenty-four hamsters. BestKeeper looks at the spread of raw Ct values, NormFinder separates variation within and between groups, and geNorm asks how constant a gene stays relative to all the others. A gene can look steady on one of those yardsticks and wobble on another.

The consensus rank squeezes these disagreements into one number, which is convenient, and the arithmetic is simple enough to check by hand. PPIA in the bacterin group is fourth by ΔCt, third by BestKeeper, seventh by NormFinder and seventh by geNorm; the geometric mean of 4, 3, 7 and 7 is 4.92, which is the value in the table, and it lands PPIA sixth overall in that group. It also hides that the four methods are chewing on the same Ct values from the same four animals, so three of four pointing the same way is not four independent experiments agreeing. The authors acknowledge that four animals per group is a small sample and lean on the low variation between replicate Ct values to defend the robustness of the result. I accept part of that defence. Low replicate variation does not make up for few animals, but it does tell you the noise is not coming from the pipette.

There is also a quirk at the bottom of geNorm that shows up in the tables. In the Salmonella group PPIA and PPIB both get an M value of 0.13 and share first place, because geNorm cannot separate the final pair of genes. That comes from how the algorithm works, but a reader who does not know it could take the two as equally good.

## The gene that looked fine in the controls

The most instructive part of the paper, to me, is not the pooled ranking but the group-by-group one. ACTB is eleventh, last, in four of the six groups: bacterin, BCG, Salmonella and Al(OH)3. In the infected group it is eighth. In the PBS controls it is fourth, and by NormFinder it shares first place. YWHAZ runs the opposite way, first in the aluminium hydroxide group and last in the PBS group. PPIA is first in BCG, Salmonella and infection, yet sixth in the bacterin group.

This pattern has a practical consequence. In most designs the control group is the calibrator for the ΔΔCt calculation. If you check a reference gene only in untreated animals, ACTB would look perfectly respectable, and the trouble would appear only after the treatment, in the very samples you care about. That reading is my own inference; the authors do not put it this way. They attribute the differences to the immune context: bacterin and aluminium hydroxide drive mainly humoral responses, BCG and attenuated Salmonella drive Th1-weighted cellular ones, and active leptospirosis produces a dysregulated systemic inflammation. The cellular composition of blood differs under each, and if a gene's behaviour depends on that composition, "stable" has to be re-earned for every setting.

The RNA-seq-derived candidates mostly lagged behind the classical and non-conventional genes. The authors suggest why. Leukocyte activation produces reactive oxygen species and perturbs antioxidant enzymes, and proliferating immune cells change their demand for ribosomal proteins and immunophilins. A gene that looks constant in solid tissue or at rest may not stay constant inside an activated leukocyte.

## What ten-fold means in cycles

The number itself comes from infected animals. TNF-α was calculated by ΔΔCt with the mean of the PBS group set to 1. Normalised to ACTB, the value was about ten times higher than when normalised to the most stable gene, and the difference was significant at p ≤ 0.01. Converting that to Ct units, a tenfold factor is roughly 3.3 cycles, since two raised to 3.3 is about ten. Three cycles of disagreement between two normalisers is not a rounding issue in a qPCR experiment.

The arithmetic has a direction too. In ΔΔCt each sample's reference Ct is subtracted from its target Ct, and then the infected group is compared with the controls. If the ACTB-based fold change is about ten times larger than the PPIA-based one, then in infected blood the Ct of ACTB must have risen, relative to the Ct of PPIA, by about 3.3 cycles more than in controls. Put differently, next to PPIA, ACTB looks less abundant in infected blood. The authors' own reasoning points the other way, towards actin being switched up when cells are activated. The two stories do not quite fit: either PPIA went up, or something else is pulling ACTB down. The raw Ct values are in the supplement, so I could not tell which. Still, the sign is a reminder that the tidy explanation for ACTB's failure has not been measured.

Timing deserves a careful read as well. Vaccinated hamsters were sampled 21 days after the booster, when the systemic response has settled. Infected hamsters were sampled in the acute phase, on the day each reached its clinical endpoint, and not every animal will have reached it on the same day. So the difference in ACTB behaviour between infection and the vaccine groups mixes the type of immune response with the timing of sampling. The authors discuss this themselves. Their choice of TNF-α as the target rests on earlier reports of increased expression in infected hamsters, which means they tested normalisation under exactly the condition where a problem was most likely to show.

A geNorm pairwise variation analysis indicated that three reference genes were enough (V below the conventional 0.15). Normalising to the three most stable genes together did not differ significantly from normalising to PPIA alone, while ACTB differed from both. The authors conclude that a single gene, PPIA, is defensible when practical limits rule out three.

The limit I would state plainly is that nobody knows the right answer here. If TNF-α had also been quantified independently, by digital PCR or at the protein level, one could say which normalisation sits closer to the truth. What the paper has is two facts: ACTB splits from PPIA, and PPIA agrees with a three-gene combination. The second fact is reassuring, because several independent genes landing in the same place is harder to explain by chance than one gene going astray. But it is still the same twenty-four hamsters, not an independent validation cohort. The animals that selected the reference gene also tested the selection.

## The same gene in other tubes

Two small studies published in recent weeks fill in the picture from other sides. Ito and Hasegawa (2026) followed PMA-differentiated THP-1 macrophages under LPS and found that the best pair of reference genes depended on how long the stimulation lasted: RPLP0 with HPRT1 after three hours, GAPDH with PPIB after twenty-four, while B2M was the worst in both. Same cell line, same LPS concentration, different protocol, different winners. Oubaqui and colleagues (2026) tested ten genes in twenty breast samples, thirteen tumours and seven adjacent normal tissues, and found TBP, PUM1 and RER1 on top, with ACTB and GAPDH at the bottom. GAPDH plays three different parts across these three studies: second overall in hamster blood, half of the best pair in THP-1 cells at 24 hours, and among the worst in breast tissue. Genes are not good or bad in general. They are good or bad in a given tissue under a given condition.

None of this is new. Vandesompele and colleagues showed in 2002, using ten control genes across human tissues, that relying on a single gene produced large errors in a sizeable share of samples, and that the geometric mean of several well-chosen genes was a safer normalisation factor (Vandesompele et al., 2002). Andersen and colleagues' NormFinder estimates the variance between sample subgroups separately and is less sensitive to co-regulated candidates (Andersen et al., 2004). The MIQE guidelines of 2009 asked that qPCR experiments be published with complete methodological detail (Bustin et al., 2009), and the 2025 revision, MIQE 2.0, restates current recommendations for normalisation and quality control (Bustin et al., 2025). The hamster paper adds nothing to the principle. It puts a price on ignoring it, in a single tenfold number.

For anyone working with blood the open question is how much cell composition decides which gene looks stable. If a full blood count had been reported alongside the Ct values from the same hamsters, one could set the drop of ACTB in the infected groups directly against the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio. Several of the grouped rankings would also be easier to judge with the raw Ct values of all eleven genes in the main text rather than the supplement, so that a reader could see which direction each gene moved. As it stands, there is a reasonable story about why ACTB fails and no evidence for it.
