Whether a cow or ewe stays pregnant is decided in the uterus in the first two weeks or so after fertilisation. If the embryo cannot tell the mother "I am here" within that short window, the corpus luteum regresses, progesterone falls and the pregnancy ends. For decades, veterinary reproductive biology textbooks have named a single carrier for that message: interferon tau (IFNT), secreted by the embryo. But what if this "indispensable" signal is not indispensable at all?

A study published in *Nature Communications* in October 2026 tested exactly that question genetically. Ali and colleagues at Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich deleted every functional copy of the IFNT genes from the bovine genome, cloned embryos from these cells and transferred them into synchronised heifers. The result was unexpected: embryos without IFNT signalled pregnancy, maintained the corpus luteum and were born as healthy calves (Ali et al., 2026). This post walks through what the study did, what it showed and what it has not yet shown.

## The classical model: why was IFNT considered the only signal?

In ruminants, maternal recognition of pregnancy (MRP) relies on the embryo sending a message that stops a cyclic hormonal event in the uterus. Late in the oestrous cycle of a non-pregnant cow, the uterus produces oxytocin-driven pulses of prostaglandin F₂α (PGF₂α), which destroy the corpus luteum (luteolysis). In pregnancy this machinery must be silenced. Spencer and Bazer (2004) summarise the mechanism: in sheep, the conceptus trophoblast produces interferon tau, which inhibits transcription of the oestrogen receptor alpha gene directly and the oxytocin receptor gene indirectly, preventing the endometrial luteolytic mechanism from developing.

The history of IFNT explains the strength of this model. The embryonic protein that extended the life of the corpus luteum in sheep was first known as ovine trophoblast protein-1 (oTP-1). Imakawa and colleagues (1987) derived its amino acid sequence from a cloned complementary DNA and showed that the protein was most probably an interferon-alpha-like molecule. Later work established that this type I interferon is produced only by the ruminant trophectoderm; in cattle it is secreted from about day 7 to day 28 of gestation, with a peak around days 18–20 (Ali et al., 2026).

IFNT does more than prevent luteolysis. It switches on a broad network of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) in the endometrium, which contribute to immune regulation and to uterine receptivity for the embryo. In their review, Forde and Lonergan (2017) describe the importance of IFNT for MRP in ruminants as unequivocal; the questions they list as open concern, for example, the threshold level of IFNT needed to maintain pregnancy. In other words, the field was not asking whether IFNT is required, only how much of it is enough.

One point deserves emphasis: much of the evidence behind the model was correlative or gain-of-function. What happens when IFNT is measured or supplied was well documented; whether pregnancy truly fails when IFNT is absent had never been tested genetically. Ali and colleagues point precisely to this gap (Ali et al., 2026).

## Study design: deleting a whole gene family

IFNT is not a single gene but a locus made of closely related copies. In cattle, at least three validated coding sequences map to chromosome 8, all encoding the same 195-amino-acid protein. A conventional single-gene knockout would therefore not have been enough. The investigators used CRISPR-Cas9 with two guide RNAs (sgRNAs), delivered as ribonucleoprotein complexes with recombinant Cas9 protein into bovine fetal fibroblasts by electroporation. Single-cell clones were screened with three separate polymerase chain reactions (PCR) that target different parts of the IFNT locus, and two clones (22 and 34) lacking all detectable functional copies were selected (Ali et al., 2026).

The deletion was not verified at the DNA level alone. During long-term in vitro culture of embryos, IFNT release into the medium was tested by three independent approaches: an antiviral (cytopathic-effect inhibition) bioassay, Western blotting and mass-spectrometric quantification of IFNT-specific peptides. Wild-type embryos gave strong antiviral activity and clear IFNT signals, whereas IFNT-null embryos showed neither activity nor peptides (Ali et al., 2026). This layered validation is one of the strongest features of the study: the knockout claim rests not on "the gene is gone" but on "the protein is gone and the function is gone".

Embryos were made by somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT): the nucleus of a knockout fibroblast was transferred into an enucleated bovine oocyte. SCNT wild-type embryos and in vitro fertilisation (IVF) embryos served as controls. High-quality blastocysts were selected on day 7 and transferred into oestrus-synchronised heifers; one group was slaughtered on day 18 to collect conceptuses and endometrial biopsies, while others were followed by ultrasonography.

Three kinds of readouts were considered together: embryo development (blastocyst rates, elongation), the maternal molecular response (endometrial proteome) and the clinical outcome of pregnancy (extended luteal phase, ultrasound, birth). This is far more convincing than relying on a single endpoint.

## Findings: an embryo without interferon, a uterus without interferon, yet a pregnancy

**In the dish.** Across five independent sessions, 212 knockout and 144 wild-type embryos were produced. Fusion rates (median 89.3% versus 88.5%), cleavage rates (72.0% versus 54.5%) and blastocyst rates (52.7% versus 40.0%) did not differ significantly between groups. Loss of IFNT therefore does not impair preimplantation development in culture (Ali et al., 2026).

**Day-18 conceptuses.** Conceptus recovery was 100% in recipients of knockout embryos and 50% for wild type, yielding four knockout and three wild-type conceptuses. Mean conceptus length was 34.0 cm in the knockout group and 36.6 cm in wild type (p = 0.71), so elongation was equivalent. Proteomic and transcriptomic profiles of the conceptuses did not differ significantly either, and there was no sign of a compensatory rise in another interferon. Prostaglandin levels and the expression of prostaglandin E synthases (PTGES, PTGES2, PTGES3) were similar between genotypes (Ali et al., 2026).

**The mother's response.** The endometrial proteome, by contrast, showed the expected difference: endometria associated with knockout conceptuses had markedly lower levels of proteins in pathways linked to IFNT, including interferon signalling, ISG15-mediated antiviral mechanisms and type I interferon responses. The uterus never displayed the classical pregnancy signature, and still the embryo elongated.

**Pregnancy outcomes.** In an independent series, ten heifers received two (n = 8) or three (n = 2) knockout blastocysts. Two recipients returned to oestrus within 20–21 days; the remaining eight (80%) showed prolonged luteal phases. Ultrasonography confirmed twin pregnancies in three recipients on days 40, 47 and 49. One twin pregnancy was terminated at day 50 and another at day 75 to examine placental development; at day 75 the uterus was of appropriate size, about 60 caruncles and about 72 cotyledons were counted, and the placentomes looked morphologically normal. The third twin pregnancy went to term, and two healthy female calves were born on 26 February 2026; both were confirmed IFNT-null by genotyping. In addition, in a round where four recipients each received a single knockout blastocyst, one pregnancy was detected on day 36, reconfirmed on day 46 and was ongoing when the paper was written. The overall ultrasound-confirmed pregnancy rate across all cohorts was 4/14 (28.6%) (Ali et al., 2026).

A pregnancy from a single embryo matters, because it weakens the objection that success with two embryos could reflect complementary back-up signals from two conceptuses.

## Critical appraisal: what does it prove, and what does it not?

The study is striking, but concluding that "IFNT does nothing" would be wrong, and the authors do not say so. Several limitations should be kept in mind.

*The sample is small.* The pregnancy rate is 4/14, and the day-18 analysis rests on four knockout and three wild-type conceptuses. Of three twin pregnancies, only one went to term, giving two calves. Statistical power is low, but for a qualitative conclusion ("birth is possible without IFNT") these numbers suffice: one live calf is enough to refute a general claim of necessity.

*Cloning may be a confounder.* Developmental abnormalities are well known in SCNT embryos, and some fetuses in this study showed cloning-associated defects. Mansouri-Attia and colleagues (2009) had shown that the endometrium adjusts its gene expression at implantation according to the origin of the embryo, and that metabolic and immune pathways were significantly altered in SCNT pregnancies. Cloned embryos are therefore not a "physiological" reference; yet because wild-type SCNT controls were included, the difference between knockout and control remains interpretable.

*One species and one genetic background.* The results apply to cattle, and all embryos were derived from a single fetal cell line (two clones); all embryos and calves were female. The authors note that complete IFNT deletion has not yet been done in sheep and that this is a logical next step.

*The alternative signal is still a hypothesis.* The authors suggest that an embryo-derived prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂) pathway could protect the corpus luteum. Their support is that PGE₂ accumulation in the day-18 conceptus is similar in knockout and wild type, and the synthetic enzymes are equally expressed. That is an observed association, not proof that PGE₂ is required, and the paper itself states that formal loss-of-function studies are needed.

*The time window is limited.* The endometrial response was examined only on day 18; later changes, or subtle contributions of IFNT to placental development, infection defence and pregnancy quality, are invisible to this design. The authors discuss that IFNT might play auxiliary roles such as antiviral defence, and that the evolution of IFNT-evasion strategies in bovine viral diarrhoea virus may point in that direction.

*Compensation cannot be fully excluded.* Similar conceptus proteomes and transcriptomes argue against a simple compensatory mechanism inside the embryo, but they do not rule out a different compensation in the mother or at the embryo–maternal interface. Day-18 samples and bulk tissue measurements can hide small but important cell-type-specific changes. The real contribution of the work is therefore a solid negative result, "pregnancy is possible without IFNT"; what takes its place is the subject of a separate research programme.

## Broader context: parallel clues in sheep and a paradigm shift

This is not a surprise confined to cattle. Davies and colleagues (2026) reached a related conclusion with sheep lacking the IFNAR2 subunit of the type I interferon receptor. IFNAR2⁻/⁻ lambs were produced by SCNT; remarkably, two IFNAR2⁻/⁻ ewes of about nine months, bred to an IFNAR2⁺/⁻ ram, became pregnant and gave birth to healthy lambs in May 2023. According to the authors, this shows that IFNAR, and presumably IFNT, are not required for the establishment or normal progression of pregnancy, although the existence of another IFNT receptor, judged unlikely, cannot be completely excluded.

The two studies complement each other. The sheep model deletes the *receptor*; the parents had intact IFNT genes, so the embryos produced IFNT and only reception of the signal was blocked. The cattle model deletes the *ligand* itself. Together they show that the pathway can be cut from both ends and pregnancy can still be established. According to Ali and colleagues, the cattle model is the first direct, ligand-level test of the necessity of the IFNT–IFNAR axis.

These findings do not demolish a model that has stood since the late 1980s, but they narrow its scope. The molecule described by Imakawa and colleagues (1987) is a genuine embryo-derived interferon with real antiluteolytic activity, and the body of data reviewed by Forde and Lonergan (2017) links it to pregnancy success. But being associated with success is not the same as being obligatory. The new work suggests that IFNT may have a facilitating or safeguarding role while another, perhaps prostaglandin-based, mechanism can substitute for it when needed.

This is also a caution for reproductive efficiency. According to the authors, for decades diagnostics and interventions against early pregnancy loss have focused almost exclusively on monitoring or supplementing IFNT, which may have meant targeting a parallel, non-obligatory pathway rather than the main driver. Pregnancy biomarkers based on endometrial ISG expression deserve a fresh look as well: if pregnancy can continue when the ISG signature is absent because IFNT is absent, then ISG expression may be a marker of the *presence of IFNT* rather than of the *presence of a pregnancy*. This inference is my own reading; and since IFNT deficiency is extremely rare in natural pregnancies, it need not immediately remove the practical diagnostic value of ISGs.

One more note on gene expression: the study is instructive in reading the endometrial response at the proteome level, not only for selected genes. Mansouri-Attia and colleagues (2009) showed through expression profiling that the endometrium behaves as a "biological sensor" of embryo quality; Ali and colleagues suggest that the same sensor may sit within a pregnancy-recognition circuit that can run without IFNT. Reading the two studies together is a reminder that the endometrial transcriptome is both a diagnostic window and a resource for hunting alternative signals.

## What comes next?

The questions ahead are clear:

1. **What is the alternative signal?** PGE₂ is the most-cited candidate but remains a hypothesis. Genetic models that selectively block conceptus PGE synthesis, or single-cell approaches that profile endometrial cell types separately, could answer this.
2. **What happens in sheep and other ruminants?** The authors stress that complete IFNT deletion should be tried in sheep; this will show whether the result is species-specific.
3. **What is the auxiliary role of IFNT?** If IFNT improves pregnancy success, by what mechanism and under which conditions? Immune defence and placental development are the likeliest areas.
4. **What do biomarkers mean now?** Which pregnancy-detection strategies used on farms depend on IFNT and which do not?

The take-home points: (1) Absence of embryonic IFNT in cattle did not prevent establishment of pregnancy, maintenance of the corpus luteum or birth; (2) pregnancy continued even though the uterus mounted no interferon response; (3) receptor deletion in sheep gives a similar picture; (4) the alternative mechanism is unproven and only a candidate; (5) given the small sample and cloning-related limitations, the findings are best read as a strong challenge rather than a settled new model.

For a veterinarian or a young reproductive biologist, the central message is that textbook models, however long they have been repeated, remain working hypotheses until tested genetically. CRISPR and cloning have made that test feasible even in large animals. The study also reminds us that embryo–maternal communication contains more back-up circuits than we assumed, and it asks for a fresh look at the biology of early pregnancy loss.
