On 26 February 2026, two heifer calves were born. On paper, neither should have existed. Each had gone into its mother's uterus as an embryo unable to make interferon tau (IFNT), the best-known pregnancy signal in ruminants. According to the textbook diagram, an embryo like that cannot announce itself to the mother, and the pregnancy quietly ends at around two weeks.

The calves are the most tangible result of a study published in *Nature Communications* in October 2026. Ali and colleagues at Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich deleted every functional copy of the IFNT genes from the bovine genome, cloned embryos from those cells and transferred them into heifers. The embryos established pregnancy, the corpus luteum was maintained, and two healthy calves were born (Ali et al., 2026).

My first reaction on reading it was to redraw the diagram I memorised at veterinary school. This result erases the box in its middle.

## How one protein became the signal

In cows and ewes, the fate of a pregnancy is decided roughly two weeks after fertilisation. In a non-pregnant animal, towards the end of the oestrous cycle, oxytocin drives the uterus to release pulses of prostaglandin F₂α (PGF₂α). Those pulses destroy the corpus luteum, progesterone falls, and the animal starts a new cycle. If a pregnancy is to continue, that machinery has to be switched off. In ruminants this is called maternal recognition of pregnancy.

Spencer and Bazer (2004) summarised the accepted mechanism in sheep. The embryo's trophoblast produces IFNT, which in the endometrium suppresses the oestrogen receptor alpha gene directly and the oxytocin receptor gene indirectly. A uterus that cannot respond to oxytocin cannot mount luteolytic PGF₂α pulses, and the corpus luteum survives.

The molecule's history goes back to the 1980s. The protein secreted by the sheep embryo that prolonged the life of the corpus luteum was first known as ovine trophoblast protein-1 (oTP-1). From the amino acid sequence deduced from a cloned complementary DNA, Imakawa and colleagues (1987) showed that it was most likely an interferon-alpha-like molecule. Over the following years the picture sharpened. This type I interferon is produced only by the ruminant trophectoderm; in cattle it is secreted from about day 7 to day 28 of pregnancy and peaks around days 18–20 (Ali et al., 2026).

Rescuing the corpus luteum is not all it does. IFNT switches on a large network of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) in the endometrium, some involved in immune regulation and some in preparing the uterus to accept the embryo.

In their review, Forde and Lonergan (2017) treated the importance of IFNT in this process as beyond dispute. The open question, as they saw it, was how much IFNT a pregnancy needs. The field was arguing about the threshold, not about whether the signal was required.

Look closely at what that confidence rested on, though, and a gap appears. Most of the evidence was correlational or came from adding IFNT: researchers knew what rose alongside it and what happened when it was supplied. Nobody had tested genetically whether an embryo with no IFNT at all could establish a pregnancy. Ali and colleagues place their study squarely in that gap. I suspect it stayed open for so long for a mundane reason: deleting an entire gene family in a cattle embryo and then following the pregnancy to term was, until recently, beyond what most labs could attempt.

## Three copies, two guide RNAs

The hard part is that IFNT is not one gene. In cattle, a locus on chromosome 8 holds at least three verified coding sequences, all encoding the same 195-amino-acid protein. A conventional knockout of one copy would prove nothing while the others remained.

The team used CRISPR-Cas9 with two guide RNAs (sgRNAs), assembled with recombinant Cas9 protein into ribonucleoprotein complexes and delivered by electroporation into bovine fetal fibroblasts. Clones grown from single cells were screened with three separate PCR assays aimed at different parts of the locus, and two clones confirmed to lack every functional copy were chosen: clones 22 and 34 (Ali et al., 2026).

Whenever I read a knockout paper, the first question in my head is the same: is it really all gone? Here the answer did not stop at the DNA. The embryos were kept in long-term culture, and the medium was tested for IFNT in three independent ways: an antiviral assay (IFNT protects cells from virus-induced death), a Western blot, and mass spectrometry of peptides unique to the protein. Wild-type embryos produced strong antiviral activity and a clear IFNT signal. The knockout embryos produced neither activity nor peptides.

So the claim rests on more than "the gene is gone": the protein is gone, and so is its function. That detail is what made me trust everything that followed.

The embryos were made by somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT), with the nucleus of a knockout fibroblast moved into an enucleated bovine oocyte. There were two control groups: wild-type embryos made the same way, and embryos from in vitro fertilisation (IVF). Good-quality blastocysts were selected on day 7 and transferred into heifers whose oestrus had been synchronised. One group was slaughtered on day 18 to collect conceptuses and endometrium; the others were followed by ultrasound.

In culture, missing IFNT made no difference to development. Across five sessions the team produced 212 knockout and 144 wild-type embryos; 52.7% and 40.0% reached the blastocyst stage, and the difference was not significant (Ali et al., 2026). The real test would come after transfer.

## A silent uterus, a growing embryo

Day 18 is when IFNT peaks in cattle, and it supplies the study's most striking data. From the slaughtered heifers the team recovered four knockout and three wild-type conceptuses. The knockouts averaged 34.0 cm in length and the wild types 36.6 cm (p = 0.71). At this stage the ruminant conceptus becomes filamentous and elongates rapidly, and that elongation is the most visible sign of healthy development. On that measure, the two groups did not differ.

The conceptuses' own protein and transcript profiles were similar too. The researchers looked specifically for another interferon rising to compensate for the missing one and found no sign of it. Prostaglandin levels and the expression of the prostaglandin E synthases PTGES, PTGES2 and PTGES3 were also similar between genotypes (Ali et al., 2026).

On the mother's side, the picture was completely different. In uteri carrying knockout conceptuses, endometrial proteins in IFNT-dependent processes were significantly reduced: interferon signalling, ISG15-mediated antiviral mechanisms and type I interferon response pathways. The uterus never produced what textbooks call the signature of pregnancy.

The embryo kept growing anyway.

The real question was whether those pregnancies would continue. In a separate series, ten heifers received knockout blastocysts, two of them three embryos each and eight of them two. Two animals came back into oestrus within 20–21 days. In the other eight the luteal phase was extended, meaning the corpus luteum did not regress on schedule. Ultrasound confirmed twin pregnancies in three heifers on days 40, 47 and 49. One was ended on day 50 and another on day 75 to examine the placenta; at day 75 the uterus was the expected size, with about 60 caruncles and 72 cotyledons, and the placentomes looked morphologically normal. The third twin pregnancy went to term, and genotyping confirmed that both heifer calves were IFNT knockouts (Ali et al., 2026).

Twins invite an objection: with two conceptuses in the uterus, weak signals from each could add up and cross some threshold. One result undercuts it. Of four heifers that received a single knockout blastocyst, one was found pregnant on day 36, confirmed again on day 46, and was still pregnant when the paper was written. Across all groups, 4 of 14 recipients (28.6%) had ultrasound-confirmed pregnancies.

These numbers are small, and the authors do not hide it. The day-18 comparison rests on four and three conceptuses, and only one pregnancy reached term. As a dataset, it is statistically thin. But the claim is logical rather than quantitative: to overturn "pregnancy cannot be established without IFNT", one live calf is enough. There are two.

A more serious concern is that the embryos were cloned. Developmental abnormalities in SCNT embryos are well documented, and some fetuses in this study showed cloning-related defects. Mansouri-Attia and colleagues (2009) found that the endometrium adjusts its gene expression at implantation according to how the embryo was produced, with metabolic and immune pathways clearly altered in SCNT pregnancies. A cloned embryo is not a natural reference point. Because the study included wild-type SCNT controls, however, the difference between knockouts and controls cannot be blamed on cloning itself.

One more limitation is easy to miss: every embryo came from two clones of a single fetal cell line, and all of them were female. Whether the result holds on a different genetic background, or in male embryos, these data cannot say.

## News from the sheep

On its own, this could be shelved as one lab's oddity. But the same year brought similar news from sheep.

Davies and colleagues (2026) deleted IFNAR2, a subunit of the type I interferon receptor. Those sheep were also produced by SCNT. At about nine months of age, two IFNAR2⁻/⁻ ewes were mated with an IFNAR2⁺/⁻ ram; both became pregnant and delivered healthy lambs in May 2023. The authors read this as evidence that IFNAR, and therefore IFNT signalling, is not required for pregnancy to be established and to progress, while acknowledging that another IFNT receptor, though unlikely, cannot be completely excluded.

The two studies cut the pathway at opposite ends. In the sheep, the embryos still made IFNT because their parents' IFNT genes were intact; only reception of the signal was blocked. In the cattle, the signal itself was never made. According to Ali and colleagues, theirs is the first direct test of whether the IFNT-IFNAR axis is required at the level of the ligand. When two experiments in two species, one removing the sender and one the receiver, land in the same place, the finding stops looking like a laboratory quirk.

None of this makes the molecule Imakawa and colleagues described in 1987 unimportant. IFNT really is embryo-derived and really can act against luteolysis, and the data reviewed by Forde and Lonergan link it to pregnancy success. What the new work changes is the kind of link. IFNT may behave less like a lock and more like a signal that makes the job easier and adds a margin of safety.

## PGE₂ and the questions left open

So what kept the corpus luteum alive? The authors' candidate is prostaglandin E₂ (PGE₂). At day 18, PGE₂ accumulation in the conceptus was similar in knockouts and wild types, and the synthesising enzymes were expressed at the same levels. That is an association, and the authors do not present it as proof; they write that loss-of-function studies are needed to show whether PGE₂ is actually required. My guess is that this will be the next paper: selectively silencing PGE synthesis in the conceptus and asking the same question again.

The time window is narrow as well. The uterus was examined only on day 18, using bulk tissue measurements, which can hide small changes confined to particular cell types and say nothing about subtler contributions IFNT might make later to the placenta or to the quality of the pregnancy. The authors suggest that IFNT may play a supporting role in immune defence, and they point to a telling detail: bovine viral diarrhoea virus appears to have evolved strategies to evade IFNT. A virus does not usually go to the trouble of dodging a signal that does nothing.

A complete IFNT deletion in sheep has not been done yet, and the authors see it as the next step. Since sheep are where the IFNT model was first built, that result may land even harder than this one.

What I keep coming back to is diagnosis. Endometrial ISG expression is used as a molecular marker of early pregnancy. In this study the ISG signature vanished and the pregnancy carried on. ISG expression may therefore report the presence of IFNT rather than the presence of a pregnancy. That is my own reading, and how much it matters in practice is debatable, since a complete absence of IFNT in natural pregnancies would be very rare. The authors make a broader point about early pregnancy loss: for decades, diagnostic and therapeutic strategies have focused almost exclusively on monitoring or supplementing IFNT signalling. If that signal is not required, some of that effort may have been aimed at a parallel route rather than at whatever really decides the outcome.

For anyone dealing with early embryonic loss on farms, it comes down to one question: all these years, were we measuring the pregnancy itself, or just its loudest messenger?
